The problem

Language models now solve Olympiad problems and sustain hours of autonomous work, yet their ability to adapt has not kept pace.

Remembering a fact is not the same as knowing when it matters.

Current memory systems fail in both directions. In PrefEval, preference following falls below 10% at ten turns for most evaluated models in the zero-shot setting. RPEval measures the opposite failure: models overuse irrelevant memories and distort the user's intent. Across thirteen memory-system configurations, MemTrace finds that when systems fail, the evidence was retrievable about ten times more often than it was missing. Storage and retrieval do not decide whether a memory applies, how strongly it should shape an answer, or when it should be ignored.

What is missing is not more storage alone, but an agent that continually learns from experience—deciding what a lesson means, whether it should persist, and how it should change future behavior.

AI assistants already reach hundreds of millions of people. Across those sessions, failures, tool outputs, corrections, preferences, and successful attempts form a vast potential learning signal. Today, those signals usually affect only the current context or external memory—the model's parameters stay frozen.

The pattern

The history of NLP shows a recurring shift. A capability begins as task-specific machinery; training then absorbs its decisions into a general model.

Representations, outputs, task adaptation, and parts of reasoning have already moved this way. It is Sutton’s Bitter Lesson playing out in language models: general methods that scale with computation ultimately outperform hand-designed solutions. We argue that continual learning should undergo the same shift: decisions now fixed in learning pipelines should become model behavior, expressed in language and installed by training.

The proposal

Continual learning from experience should be a capability of the model: the model should learn from feedback in language, control its learning in language, and store what it learns in its own memory, from context to weights.

We call this the language of learning: feedback and the learner's control share language. The architecture supplies the update mechanism; the semantic content of the learner's learning act determines what is retained and where. The act may be a brief reflection or a longer trajectory, and its learning tokens may interleave with task work.

The prediction

Learning tokens can create a new scaling axis: just as reasoning tokens let a model spend more computation on a difficult problem, learning tokens let it spend more computation for learning.

At matched substrate, training data, available evidence, and objective, we predict that a larger learning-token budget will improve later transfer on held-out learning situations that require diagnosis or integration, until gains saturate or interference dominates.

Recipe

Our position can be stated as five requirements:

  • Learning as capability — a general policy installed through training performs learning at test time and generalizes to learning situations not encountered during training.
  • Parametric and non-parametric memory — the learner combines in-context learning with in-weight learning through test-time plasticity; non-parametric memory comprises context and, when available, external memory.
  • Feedback in language — feedback of any type, including a binary or scalar outcome, reaches the learner as content through its language interface.
  • Control in language — the model's own open language semantically and causally determines the content, scope, and memory destination of the resulting state change.
  • Lifelong — experience-induced changes remain available across later test-time episodes in a continuing stream without a prescribed reset at episode boundaries.

Opportunities

Learning throughout deployment could change not only what an agent knows, but how it develops over its lifetime.

Compounding learningAn agent may improve not only what it can do, but how efficiently it acquires new capabilities—requiring fewer interactions, less feedback, or less computation as its lifetime progresses.
Personalization, specialization, and expertiseModels beginning from the same checkpoint could develop different skills, judgment, and strategies through work with a person, organization, or domain, reducing dependence on provider-run retraining.
Open-ended discoveryAgents shaped by different experience streams could exchange discoveries and learning strategies, turning divergent expertise into stepping stones for further learning.
