# Lecture 23 - Mathematics · notes
- [00:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=39s) Hamming: Stop occasionally to examine the background you normally take for granted. (context: He chooses mathematics, used for years without asking what it is, and compares this with asking what Bell Labs was and how its culture molded people.)
- [02:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=132s) Hamming: Treat mathematics as a tool for making thought precise: "mathematics is the language of clear thinking". (context: A department head at a party repeats that mathematics is nothing but clear thinking three times until a critic of statistics stops. Hamming prefers calling it a language; he says it works imperfectly but compares favorably with legal and tax prose.)
- [03:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=196s) Hamming: Distinguish an underlying idea from the conventions used to express it, even when calling mathematics universal. (context: He recalls Los Alamos colleagues expecting a communicating alien civilization to have something like Maxwell's equations. But aliens might distinguish reflected triangles as congruent or anti-congruent. His claim of universality is qualified by essentially and is a conjecture, not an observation of alien mathematics.)
- [07:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=451s) Hamming: Ask what your language assumes about discovery: did he discover error-correcting codes, or create them? (context: His account of Platonism puts every theorem in a timeless realm, already there at the Big Bang. He questions how that accounts for changing definitions, and notes that a rock can be a chair at a picnic without being a chair you expect in a living room. This is his criticism of the philosophy, not a settled refutation.)
- [10:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=605s) Hamming: Formal manipulation alone leaves the application unexplained: "when rigor enters meaning departs". (context: Hamming explicitly presents this as a paraphrase of Hilbert, then asks why a supposedly meaningless game, like chess, can predict planets, missiles, and atomic bombs. He is challenging an account of mathematical usefulness, not denying that formal proofs are useful.)
- [11:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=704s) Hamming: A familiar-looking proof can smuggle in an unjustified picture; knowing its conclusion is wrong is only the start of finding the error. (context: He demonstrates the false proof that every triangle is isosceles. In his explanation, the bisectors meet "outside not inside", breaking the pictured argument. He uses Hilbert's explicit treatment of intersections and betweenness to show why assumptions hidden in a drawing matter.)
- [15:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=926s) Hamming: Useful mathematics can grow backward from results you need: "you start with a body of knowledge and you work your way backward". (context: As a graduate student he wondered why, in his account, Hilbert's added postulates left Euclid's roughly 467 theorems intact. He concluded that both men chose assumptions to support results already believed. The count and historical interpretation are Hamming's; error-correcting codes supply his own example of choosing arithmetic to fit operations.)
- [16:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=989s) Hamming: Do not mistake familiar arithmetic for the only possible structure: "there isn't one bit of Truth in all of mathematics" is his deliberately sweeping challenge. (context: He contrasts ordinary addition with addition modulo two, where two flips of the same bit cancel. His target is unconditional truth about the world inferred from symbols alone; different defined operations do not make ordinary arithmetic false.)
- [19:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1178s) Hamming: Before trusting mathematics for action, develop a feeling for how it matches the application. (context: He asks whether you would fly in an airplane whose success depended on a function being Lebesgue-integrable but not Riemann-integrable, then questions how uncountably many discontinuities could match a finite physical world. This is his provocative intuition test, not a demonstration that Lebesgue integration is unsafe or physically useless; the captions garble both names.)
- [21:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1300s) Hamming: An existence claim and a way to build the thing answer different demands: "you got to show me how to construct it". (context: He sympathizes with constructivists who demand a method of making an object rather than accepting existence from consistency arguments. He also says those restrictions exclude mathematics he wants to use; this is his practical tension, not a complete account of constructive mathematics.)
- [22:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1330s) Hamming: A preferred philosophy need not describe everything you actually use; acknowledge where your practice exceeds it. (context: He leans toward intuitionism and constructivism but admits using mathematics he cannot justify under their strict bounds. His five-school survey ends without a satisfactory explanation of how mathematics guided a missile to the moon; his verdict on all five schools is his assessment.)
- [23:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1391s) Hamming: Change the viewpoint to match the job, and check the gap when returning to the application. (context: For debugging, the machine's bit patterns are the real numbers and the mathematician's numbers are "purely imaginary". When modeling the world, the mathematical numbers become the reference and machine values suffer roundoff and truncation. He still has to ask whether the computed number is what an experiment will show.)
- [25:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1549s) Hamming: Do not confuse a name with its object; "meaning comes from how things are manipulated not how words are said". (context: Lewis Carroll's puzzles about an object, its name, and the name of its name resemble Who's on First. Calling a child Charles rather than Willie scarcely changes the child. In Hamming's simulator, the subroutine's behavior defines the operation; the instruction's name alone does not.)
- [28:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1704s) Hamming: Recheck a proof when its definitions or standards change; repair may require a stronger hypothesis. (context: He describes extending the meaning of polyhedron until an old proof no longer applies, then gives a continuity claim that needs uniform convergence rather than convergence alone. His graduate-student task of patching great mathematicians' proofs leads to the provocative claim "no theorem is really proved"; this concerns changing definitions and standards, not a demonstrated defect in every present proof.)
- [29:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1765s) Hamming: A definition alone cannot transmit all its meaning; shared use and correction matter. (context: Pointing at a horse could indicate its name Charlie, its species, a quadruped, or its color. Children gradually repair misconceptions, and adults can still use the same words with different meanings. His dictionary story makes the distinction explicit: "we will no longer prescribe we will merely describe how words are used"; he corrects the offended reviewer's publication to the New Yorker.)
- [31:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1888s) Hamming: Mathematical rules are human choices that can be changed, not commandments handed down intact. (context: He jokes that Moses negotiated twenty commandments down to ten, but "adultery is still on the list". Mathematical postulates were not on those tablets at all. The joke supports choosing rules for a purpose; it does not license inconsistent deductions once rules are chosen.)
- [33:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=1980s) Hamming: Choose a distance for the distinction the problem needs, rather than automatically using familiar geometry. (context: For bit strings he counts differing positions. Recognizing a distant woman, he contrasts collecting many mismatches with the single decisive objection "she's too tall". These illustrate summed discrepancies versus a maximum discrepancy; his broad dismissal of squared differences outside physics is rhetoric, not a restriction on where squared-error methods can work.)
- [34:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2049s) Hamming: Formal reasoning has limits even when its rules are exact; adding assumptions does not promise to settle everything. (context: He uses Gödel to challenge Hilbert's hope for a sufficiently rich system to prove its own consistency, and says expanded systems admit new unprovable statements. His presentation is informal: the limits concern systems meeting the theorem's hypotheses, not every symbol system. His later suggestion that shifting word meanings lets language escape Gödel is speculation, not a proof that people transcend formal computation.)
- [38:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2298s) Hamming: Distinguish a problem no general program can solve from one whose algorithm we have not found: "not that we don't know how". (context: His example is the halting problem: no program can decide for every arbitrary input program whether it will halt. This is a limit on a universal decision procedure, not a claim that an individual program's termination can never be proved. He compares the contradiction style with the proof that the square root of two is irrational.)
- [39:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2391s) Hamming: Expect new applications to need unfamiliar mathematics and less exact analogies: error-correcting codes fit "beautifully exactly right". (context: He contrasts that fit with classical mechanics and with messier future applications. He describes successive Bell Labs generations as inheriting harder problems and uses the jump from the moon to Mars as an example. This is his expectation, not a proved trend; his 1995 doubts about NASA and Mars are predictions, not current facts.)
- [41:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2483s) Hamming: Keep the puzzle of successful prediction beside the limits of your theory: "not always I'm not saying we're always right". (context: He recalls reconstructing a failed White Sands missile shot from equations and a desk calculator, calculating moonshots without learning solely through repeated full-scale shots, and predicting telephone-exchange capacity from call loads and holding times. The achievements explain why he cannot dismiss mathematics as an idle game despite lacking a satisfactory philosophy of it.)
- [42:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2579s) Hamming: Ask what probability means when repeated trials are unavailable: "you don't get more than one trial on that job". (context: He recalls checking someone else's calculation of the chance that the first atomic bomb would ignite the atmosphere. The sentence about ignition is garbled in the captions; the surrounding account identifies the catastrophic atmospheric risk. His objection is to the textbook ratio-of-successes definition as an explanation of that one-shot judgment, not a claim that probabilities for unique events are meaningless.)
- [44:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km9_rBUGYYk&t=2642s) Hamming: Do not mistake measurable attributes for the whole concept: "everything really worth knowing cannot be said". (context: AT&T asked employees hundreds of questions and processed them to compare operating companies' morale. He says the answers only partly capture the thing, which can also differ between individuals in one organization. Truth, beauty, and justice extend his point; the sweeping opening is his observation, not a proof that valuable knowledge cannot be expressed.)
