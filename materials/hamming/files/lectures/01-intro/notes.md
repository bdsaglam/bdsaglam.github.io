# Lecture 1 - Intro · notes
- [01:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=96s) Hamming: Study how significant work gets done, not just how hard people work: "Many a person I've known worked just as hard as others, but didn't have much to show for it." (context: His course uses technical examples to examine styles of thinking; he says many lessons fall between ordinary courses. At 02:39–03:40 he argues that style must be inferred from examples, since abstract advice alone sounds like "so many pious words".)
- [04:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=250s) Hamming: "I have to get you to quit your modesty": take seriously the possibility that you can do significant work, then do the work yourself. (context: He challenges each student to think: if Hamming could become a great scientist, so can I. Teachers are coaches who can criticize style but cannot run your 4-minute mile for you.)
- [05:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=312s) Hamming: Build your own style by thinking over what you hear, discussing it, and adapting what fits: "You can't copy me directly." (context: Like a painter studying under a master, you need more than an exact copy of the master's work. His successful past style would not make him successful if he were starting now; the future needs a different one.)
- [05:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=343s) Hamming: Learn from masters without copying their style: "The future wants a different style." (context: A painter first learns techniques, then studies under a master, but exact imitation will not make a great painter. Hamming tells students to take useful fragments from others, adapt them to themselves, and build a style for their own future; what made him successful would not work unchanged if he started again.)
- [07:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=468s) Hamming: When you become a leader, do not force the next generation to repeat your route to success: "what made you great is not appropriate for the next generation." (context: The "four young Turks" at Bell Labs were troublemakers who tried new methods. His boss Hendrik Bode had become famous using complex variables in network theory and distrusted Hamming's computing machines, though Hamming says computing was what needed doing.)
- [09:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=562s) Hamming: "Education is what, when, and why to do things. Training is how to do it." You need both judgment and technique. (context: Very able technical staff applied their methods to the wrong problem and the work had to be undone; others had theory but could do nothing. He wants students to keep educating themselves throughout their careers.)
- [11:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=719s) Hamming: Use rough calculations to test whether impressive claims fit together and to discover what they actually mean. (context: He compares the claims that knowledge doubles every 17 years and "90% of the scientists whoever lived are now alive." Under exponential growth, a roughly 55-year scientist lifetime makes them compatible; constructing the model forces him to define that lifetime. This checks consistency under assumptions, not the empirical truth of either claim.)
- [14:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=874s) Hamming: Project a growth claim far enough to expose where it must break: "science cannot go the way it has been" indefinitely. (context: Starting with an estimated 10,000 specialties, doubling every 17 years for 340 years gives about 10 billion specialties, which he finds implausible. He also notes that exponential growth in scientists cannot continue until everybody is a scientist; these are rough extrapolations, not forecasts.)
- [15:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=942s) Hamming: Practice back-of-the-envelope modeling on numbers you hear: it tests plausibility, strengthens memory, and keeps modeling skills ready. (context: Fermi and Shockley did this at lunch. With newspaper, TV, or radio claims, a quick model may reveal either impossible numbers or that the speaker "failed to tell you what they were talking about". He explicitly says the calculation is not really accurate and 17 years is only approximate.)
- [18:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1128s) Hamming: Against expanding and aging knowledge, "My answer to that is learning to learn." Be ready to help replace what you just learned. (context: He estimates that half of what students are taught will be obsolete in 15 years. After teaching himself vacuum-tube engineering at Bell Labs, he helped calculate transistor designs that displaced it. His growth and obsolescence figures are estimates offered in 1995, not established universal rates.)
- [20:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1222s) Hamming: Learn enough of the application to understand the problem you are computing: "You have to have background knowledge enough to penetrate jargon." (context: Trained as a mathematician without courses in numerical analysis or computers, he had to learn physics, chemistry, and parts of social and biological science to handle Bell Labs problems. Computation alone was not enough to know what those problems meant.)
- [21:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1282s) Hamming: Do not call something fundamental merely because you learned it early. Look for ideas that generate the field and survive changes in equipment. (context: He calls his two tests inadequate: deriving the rest of the field and having lasted a long time. Vacuum tubes lost their central place, while feedback and Nyquist gain formulas remained useful in other devices.)
- [21:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1314s) Hamming: Science explores what you do not know; engineering requires knowing enough to make something work. Treat this as a contrast within a blend, not a rigid boundary. (context: He says science is "exploration of what you don't know", then adds that science needs engineering and engineering needs new discoveries. The engineering sentence at 22:26 is garbled in the captions; this is a paraphrase of the contrast, not a repaired quotation.)
- [22:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1377s) Hamming: Expect pressure to develop ideas while they are still new: "When an idea is first around, you want to apply it." (context: He contrasts the old telephone monopoly, which could take its time developing a physics discovery, with pressure to turn an idea into a marketed product rapidly. This shrinking interval is one reason he gives for science and engineering growing together.)
- [24:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1470s) Hamming: Forecasting requires judgment about both the model and the field; even deliberately generous predictions can miss compounding growth. (context: After repeatedly underestimating computing demand, he decided "I will predict high", then later found he was low again. Yet he says earlier AI experts' predictions had not come true by 1995. His saying "Short-term predictions are optimistic, long-term predictions are pessimistic" is a warning, not a rule without exceptions; even a linear trend depends on which variable you choose.)
- [27:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1626s) Hamming: Use history with awareness that recollection is selective, including your own. (context: Reading Ulam's Los Alamos account, Hamming told a friend, "That isn't how I remember it." The friend said the same; before asking for his version, Hamming realized "no two people remember the same." He compares this with conflicting accident witnesses, and also warns that inventions such as computers can disconnect the future from past practice.)
- [29:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1752s) Hamming: Leave room for individual action without assuming the future is unconstrained: "the past was less determined than the historians like to make and the future is less open-ended than you would like to believe." (context: He asks how history would differ if Alexander, Napoleon, Hitler, Newton, or Einstein had died young. These are his counterfactual illustrations, not demonstrations that one person alone determines events.)
- [30:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1813s) Hamming: Ask what social and legal arrangements a technical proposal needs, as well as whether the technology can work. (context: For computer-controlled highway traffic, his question is "Who do I sue in an accident?" He predicts that social, legal, and other restraints will shape technology more than technical possibility; this is his forecast, illustrated by unresolved liability.)
- [30:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=1844s) Hamming: Choose a direction so your efforts add up: without a vision you resemble a "drunken sailor". (context: His sailor typically gets only about the square root of the number of random steps away: 100 steps give about 10. With "a pretty girl over there", a directional bias gives progress proportional to the number of steps. At 31:46–32:47 he says the exact goal matters less than having one, even becoming a great guitar player. The random walk is a career analogy, not a measured law of success.)
- [33:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2028s) Hamming: "The optimizing of the components individual courses is not optimizing a total education": look for the gaps and use knowledge across departments. (context: A hard problem of unknown type needs an answer whether it comes from chemistry or physics. Though formally in mathematics at Bell Labs, he did statistics, computing, physics, and chemistry; departments organize work but do not define the boundaries of useful knowledge.)
- [36:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2184s) Hamming: Match work to human strengths and limits; reliable repetition and rapid control are poor places to rely on human flexibility. (context: Even expert bowlers rarely repeat the same throw perfectly. In his aircraft example, a computer corrects instability every millisecond while the pilot handles broad planning. His sweeping claim that humans cannot be reliable is rhetoric illustrated by these tasks, not a claim that people never perform reliably.)
- [37:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2245s) Hamming: Design rare-event monitoring around attention that will not fade: "freedom from boredom" is a practical machine advantage. (context: A person asked to watch a reactor dial for three or four uneventful years may miss the moment it moves; a computer can keep watching. At 38:28–39:29 he adds that rapid control can exceed human information bandwidth, while explicitly separating bandwidth from judgment. His 50–60 bits-per-second figure is presented as a general belief, not a universal measured limit.)
- [39:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2369s) Hamming: Compare the whole burden of using people and machines, including retraining, hazardous conditions, and management. (context: A person must unlearn old habits and practice new ones; with a computer, "I change the program." He also contrasts life support and radiation risks with machine deployment, and lists squabbles, leave, pensions, and egos among management burdens. This is his case for automation, not a claim that programming or operating machines has no costs.)
- [42:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2526s) Hamming: Notice when automation improves the process that builds the machines themselves. (context: On a computer company's board, he saw more than half the computers coming down the production line taken to mechanize that line, reducing production costs. He also recounts a friend's customized computer order assembled overnight by machines. These are his reported company experiences, not industry-wide production figures.)
- [43:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2619s) Hamming: "It's not the achievement of the goal that really is the best part. It's the struggle. The struggle to success is what makes you what you will be." (context: He bases this view on reading, studying lives, and talking with people about happiness. At 44:11–44:41 he explicitly calls the value of striving for excellence an opinion, not a fact.)
- [44:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2651s) Hamming: "Consider the kind of person you wish to be in your old age. And start now being that kind of a person." (context: He says you will be "stuck with yourself" and have less ability to change then than now. This is his advice about shaping a life, which he labels opinion rather than fact.)
- [44:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2681s) Hamming: Do not equate a good life with adding up pleasant moments: "All the laughter did not make the movie funny at all." (context: As a child he laughed through a movie, then agreed with his friend afterward that it had not been very funny. He uses that contrast to argue that momentary pleasure need not add up to a satisfying life. His proposed route is deliberate self-management and worthwhile goals; the closing Socratic refrain is "The unexamined life is not worth living.")
- [44:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AD4b-52jtos&t=2681s) Hamming: Do not judge a life by adding up its pleasant moments: "All the laughter did not make the movie funny at all." (context: As a child he laughed throughout a movie, then agreed with his friend afterward that it was not very funny. He uses this distinction between momentary reactions and the whole experience to argue for managing the person you become rather than pursuing each pleasant moment. This is his opinion about a good life; he closes with Socrates' "The unexamined life is not worth living.")
