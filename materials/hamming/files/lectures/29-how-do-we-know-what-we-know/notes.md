# Lecture 29 - How Do We Know What We Know · notes
- [02:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=126s) Hamming: Distinguish certainty within assumptions from certainty about the world: "if you change the assumptions things are different". (context: He contrasts the Greeks' confidence in geometry with mathematics based on chosen rules and assumptions. His sweeping claim that there is no truth in mathematics expresses his philosophical position; conditional mathematical proof is not thereby useless.)
- [05:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=342s) Hamming: Learning can build a workable web of meanings without an infallible starting point: "a dictionary has no first word". (context: A baby repeats sounds that get favorable responses, but even Mama does not always work. Like mutually defining dictionary words, meanings develop together and are revised when they conflict; this is his model of learning, not a complete account of language acquisition.)
- [07:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=436s) Hamming: Successful performance does not guarantee understanding, and inability to explain does not mean inability to know how. (context: His Harvard-trained mathematician friend could carry out the calculus procedure without understanding it. Bicycle riding, skating, and swimming supply the reverse warning: people can know how to act without putting that knowledge into words. The captions garble the calculus procedure, so it is not named here.)
- [08:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=537s) Hamming: Do not assume an example communicates its intended category. (context: Pointing at a horse might name that individual, its color, or the class of four-legged animals. Running, colors running in the wash, and running for office likewise force a child to infer different meanings. Hamming warns that some mistaken interpretations may persist and that revising them becomes harder with age.)
- [10:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=631s) Hamming: Expert practice and an explicit account of that practice are different: "a scientist knows science like a fish knows hydrodynamics". (context: His high-school teachers gave incompatible descriptions of science, and what they actually did differed again. The fish analogy challenges an expert's automatic authority to explain scientific method; it does not deny practical scientific competence.)
- [11:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=661s) Hamming: Data collection and interpretation already assume a view of what matters: "experiments are Theory loaded all the way through". (context: Collecting the room's color and the next bird's species at random would not by itself produce a theory. Some prior question or model guides which observations to gather and how to interpret them; those assumptions must remain open to correction.)
- [11:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=694s) Hamming: Examine beliefs acquired by imitation as well as those deliberately taught. (context: Children absorb campfire stories, employees learn an organization's behavior without explicit rules, and old hippies keep the habits of their formative years. His observation that older women's clothing reflects college fashions is explicitly tentative: he says he may be kidding himself. Familiarity is not evidence of truth.)
- [13:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=788s) Hamming: Sequence and correlation do not by themselves identify a cause: "does night cause day or day cause night"? (context: He links river height with calls at a telephone exchange: rain prompts calls for cabs or notices of delay, and also makes the river rise. The calls do not raise the river; a shared cause explains the association. The river's name is unclear in the captions.)
- [15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=913s) Hamming: Keep a name or resemblance separate from the thing it represents: "Charles is the name of the child". (context: Burning a written name is supposed to harm its bearer; resemblance-based medicine is supposed to transfer a property from food or remedy to the patient. He calls this name magic and sympathetic magic. These are his examples of confusing a representation or similarity with a causal connection.)
- [16:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=987s) Hamming: Test how much confidence actually supports a claim of absolute certainty. (context: His deliberately extreme challenge asks a supposedly certain person to sign away all their money and agree to suicide if wrong; they suddenly hesitate. He pairs this with groups who sold their possessions for a predicted end of the world and then had to start over. This is his rhetorical test of professed certainty, not advice to make such a wager.)
- [18:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1080s) Hamming: A finite run of successes does not guarantee the next physical case: "no finite number of cases can guarantee the next one". (context: Dropping balls does not by itself establish an unrestricted law; a released balloon rises and forces the conditions to be stated more carefully. Mathematical induction proves claims within specified assumptions, while repeated experiments alone do not supply that kind of proof.)
- [19:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1175s) Hamming: Before settling a technical disagreement, ask each person to say what their words mean. (context: He slows down two people arguing in a laboratory and often finds different interpretations of the same terms. Once the ambiguity is exposed, they can ask which interpretation an experiment supports rather than continue an argument whose apparent agreement on words conceals disagreement on meaning.)
- [20:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1207s) Hamming: Do not let a yes-or-no vocabulary erase a meaningful middle ground. (context: Tall and short leave people who are neither; Hamming says language handles this poorly and can constrain thought. He also rejects the stronger claim that all thought is verbal: knowing something that one cannot yet put into words is a familiar counterexample.)
- [21:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1270s) Hamming: Keep an account answerable to the event: "the words tend to replace the event". (context: Repeatedly telling a vacation story can freeze the memory into the telling. After witnessing a car accident, he gave the drivers his details, then wrote his own account at home without first discussing it with others. He tried to make the event determine the words rather than let a rehearsed account determine what he remembered.)
- [22:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1333s) Hamming: Agreement with observations need not select one unique explanation. (context: He compares several formulations of quantum mechanics with analytic and Euclidean geometry: different approaches can cover the same phenomena while serving different uses. His coding example, where one plus one is zero, also shows why apparently self-evident statements need their mathematical setting specified; it does not contradict ordinary integer arithmetic.)
- [23:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1395s) Hamming: Use a thought experiment to expose an assumption's consequences, then check it experimentally: "when are two bodies one"? (context: He imagines Galileo asking whether a heavy falling body slows when split, then speeds up if the pieces touch or are joined by string, rope, or glue. This is explicitly Hamming's imagined reconstruction, not a verified account of Galileo's discovery. The comparison assumes ideal free fall without drag; reasoning motivates the law rather than replacing empirical tests.)
- [25:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1551s) Hamming: Remember the limits of the frame through which you observe: "we always see things through a window". (context: The window filters physical frequencies, but it is also intellectual: people are prepared to accept some kinds of ideas and not others. He compares scientific correction to a child revising meanings when an interpretation stops working; a progressively more consistent account still carries no guarantee of final correctness.)
- [26:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1615s) Hamming: A theory can lose its claim to universality while remaining the right tool in its tested range. (context: Classical mechanics remains useful in the middle ground even after quantum mechanics and relativity change the larger picture. Hamming's claim that all previous theories proved wrong is his sweeping formulation; his own example distinguishes limited validity from total practical failure.)
- [27:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1679s) Hamming: Treat standards of proof and reasoning as things to examine, not inherited guarantees. (context: He says a graduate student must tighten gaps in Gauss's polynomial-root proof because standards of rigor rise. He then challenges automatic either-or reasoning through the particle-or-wave example. These are his arguments for examining assumptions, not a demonstration that valid formal logic can yield any conclusion.)
- [29:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1740s) Hamming: Match the strength of scientific language to the evidence: "at present science believes". (context: He prefers this qualification to saying science has proved an empirical claim, while admitting the wording is tiresome. The child-learning analogy supports his positive alternative: keep eliminating contradictions and improving explanations without claiming complete understanding.)
- [30:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1832s) Hamming: Practical success can justify relying on knowledge that is still revisable: "crummy old engineering knowledge" got people to the Moon and back. (context: Against prayer, ESP, or pyramid power as imagined routes to Kennedy's lunar goal, he points to the engineering that actually worked. His skepticism about certainty coexists with reliance on tested methods. He also says human welfare can matter more than engineering; technical success alone does not settle every social choice.)
- [32:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=1956s) Hamming: Judge promises by delivered results while leaving room for new evidence. (context: Polywater, cold fusion, and the AI promises he had seen illustrate claims that, in his assessment, had not delivered their advertised results by 1995. He immediately concedes he cannot prove success will not come tomorrow and even acknowledges the circularity in his own claim that science proves nothing. His historical assessment is not a verdict on later AI or a proof of impossibility.)
- [33:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=2020s) Hamming: Recheck an old impossibility judgment when its conditions change: "I merely remember it can't be done". (context: He rejected the FFT's butterfly steps as impractical on his card-programmed calculators, then carried that conclusion over after getting an internally programmed machine that could have handled them. He says he forgot that the technology had changed. The reverse also matters: closer examination tomorrow can overturn what seems successful today.)
- [35:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=2116s) Hamming: Ask whether a method's successes cover the kind of problem you actually face. (context: He questions reductionism when taking a system apart destroys what matters about the whole, and contrasts justice with the legalities of a contemporary Los Angeles trial. At 38:24 he distinguishes loosely interconnected parts and highly repeated systems from the difficult territory between them. His broad doubts about biology and science's ability to address truth, beauty, and justice are his judgments, not established limits on all future science.)
- [37:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=2241s) Hamming: Notice when repetition increases confidence without increasing evidence: "wishful thinking is one of the biggest curses man has to put up with". (context: At Bell Labs he saw a speculative possibility turn, through repeated enthusiastic talk, into something people believed could be done, with no new evidence. Wanting a result, or wanting certain truth itself, can quietly replace the question of what supports it.)
- [39:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZEjt5PerpeY&t=2366s) Hamming: Use other people's hard-earned lessons to make deliberate choices about your own life. (context: Introducing the final lecture, he says the course elaborates his research advice but could equally concern life: he claims one life to lead and asks what is sensible to do with it. Because he had to discover much of the advice for himself, he expects students who are told it to be able to do better; that is his challenge, not a guaranteed outcome.)
