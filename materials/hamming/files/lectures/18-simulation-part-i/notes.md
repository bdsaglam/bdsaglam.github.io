# Lecture 18 - Simulation, Part I · notes
- [00:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=4s) Hamming: Simulation answers "what if": use it to compare proposed actions or reconstruct what may have happened. (context: He includes building something, trying a different organization, and investigating a past disaster. The same question can guide a design or test a possible explanation.)
- [01:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=97s) Hamming: A simulation can expose quantities that are hard to measure, but precise computed numbers still depend on the model. (context: He contrasts tracking a missile at White Sands with having five-digit positions and velocities inside a simulation. His claim of more accurate answers concerns access to the simulated trajectory; the later warnings about bad equations prevent treating those digits as proof of real-world accuracy.)
- [04:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=287s) Hamming: Reliable simulation needs application experts who can supply and question the equations: "I wasn't going to sim without some numbers". (context: At Los Alamos he relied on hydrodynamics experts. Asked to simulate ecology, he requested numbers relating rainfall and sunshine to growth; when none were available, he declined and the requesters found someone else.)
- [08:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=535s) Hamming: Look for enough repeated work to justify programming: "if you don't have a great deal" of repetition, "you just calculate it once and you're done". (context: The bomb calculation repeats the same operations for each shell and time step; weather models repeat them for air blocks. He is making an effort-versus-reuse judgment about programming, not proving that one-off computations should never be automated.)
- [09:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=580s) Hamming: Ask early whether small errors will grow enough to defeat the answer you need. Stability depends on the question and time scale. (context: He contrasts the bomb design with weather, using the butterfly-in-Japan story at 06:21 as an illustration of sensitivity, not a measured forecast. Analog-computer feedback paths prompted him to ask about stability before running; recurring seasons do not imply predictable daily weather or rule out ice ages.)
- [12:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=736s) Hamming: A promising result should trigger a check of the assumptions: "if you're going to change the wing size by a factor of three those formulas can't be much good". (context: His Nike trials favored wings one third the original size and a vertical launch to escape dense air. Thinking in his motel, he realized the supplied formulas were probably local linear approximations. He called Bell Labs, returned for new formulas, and says the smaller wings and vertical launch survived the revised calculations.)
- [14:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=891s) Hamming: Leave time to acquire a feeling for the system: "fewer answers understood" can beat a flood of output. (context: Runs took about fifty minutes to an hour in his lecture account. Watching the plotters, he could almost feel the missile's drag while turning. He wonders whether minute-long runs would have kept him shuffling papers instead; he explicitly presents this as his belief, not a demonstrated effect of slow machines.)
- [15:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=954s) Hamming: Do not let inherited formulas become authority that replaces observation: simulation risks a return to "Medieval scholasticism". (context: He invokes Galileo's objection to reading Aristotle instead of looking at nature. He recalls predicting that Bell Labs would move from nine lab experiments out of ten to nine computer experiments out of ten; expensive new lab benches became programmers' desks before the doors were fitted. These are his historical recollections, not independently checked statistics.)
- [17:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1077s) Hamming: Explore broadly on the machine, then measure the crucial cases: "our formulas aren't all right they're sometimes bad". (context: At 17:26 he claims that in many areas 99% of experiments are on a machine, followed by one or two laboratory checks. The lesson is to use computation to select informative real experiments, not dispense with them; the 99% is his broad contemporary claim, not a verified rate.)
- [19:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1141s) Hamming: Start with the simplest simulation that keeps the essential effects, gain insight, then add detail. (context: A simple atmosphere revealed the benefit of leaving dense air quickly; many realistic atmospheres at the start could have hidden it. Managers wanted a detailed model immediately because one would eventually be needed. He argues for adding layers afterward, including morning, night, winter, and summer conditions before the final design.)
- [21:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1295s) Hamming: Use your access to the evolving calculation to improve the design, rather than merely evaluate a fixed one: "I could calculate a taper". (context: In the travelling-wave tube, energy moves from a faster electron beam to a slower signal. As their speeds converge, amplification ends and extending the tube adds noise. He realized that changing the tube's shape could keep the beam faster and extract more energy.)
- [22:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1328s) Hamming: Check what the approximation throws away; an "active aggressive mind" can improve a simulation even among experts. (context: In some travelling-wave-tube models, the omitted nonlinear component was larger than the retained linear component: "they were neglecting more than they were looking at". The experts disliked the criticism, but it stopped useless computing. His contrast with merely doing what one is told is an appeal to judgment, not permission to ignore domain expertise.)
- [23:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1390s) Hamming: Use an impossible ideal case to anchor understanding: "I can run a beam with no charge on it". (context: He removes the particle-repulsion term to compute a limiting beam case that cannot be built in the laboratory. It fixes the end of a family of curves. Like the ideal gas he discusses afterward, the deliberate fiction helps interpret realistic cases without becoming a claim about a buildable device.)
- [29:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1764s) Hamming: Learn a field's language before working with its experts: "I was not going to become a chemist" but had to master the jargon. (context: Before a chemical-abstracts advisory meeting, he read introductory organic and inorganic chemistry textbooks. Earlier he distinguishes useful precision, such as the physicist's meaning of work, from jargon used to exclude outsiders. His caveman groups of 25–100 and Christmas-card-list story is his speculative explanation of that exclusion, not an established cognitive limit.)
- [30:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=1826s) Hamming: Shared jargon can conceal that nobody has agreed on the meaning: "will you tell me whether salary is amplitude or power"? (context: Engineers spent lunch discussing two- and three-decibel salary raises. His question exposed disagreement about using 10 log or 20 log; in his words, they had not known what they were talking about "the whole damn lunch". Learn jargon, but check what it denotes even among insiders.)
- [34:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2075s) Hamming: Make a domain expert inspect what the program actually computes: "it's perfectly reasonable what I did but I was wrong". (context: He insisted on a review of absolute-binary code for a 28-equation Navy problem. He interpreted a bound as voltage limiting; his colleague meant a fin hitting a physical stop. Which limited value fed back into subsequent computation changed maneuverability. Hamming doubts ordinary flight checks would have caught it; plausible mathematics and specialist programmers did not remove the need for domain review.)
- [37:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2235s) Hamming: An apparently impossible model may admit a useful approximation if you exploit its structure. (context: For telephone-exchange blocking probabilities he had an infinite chain of equations but only about twelve integrators. Treating it as an impedance line, he used the last computed terms to estimate feedback from the missing tail. He jokes that because the answers were loved he must have been right; popularity is his anecdotal reception of the result, not an accuracy check.)
- [38:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2332s) Hamming: Check stability across the whole chain, not only within each component: two stability problems can coexist. (context: Each microwave relay could recover from a disturbance over time while passing a larger disturbance to the next tower. His "space stabilization" question was whether a pulse grew or decayed while crossing the continent. Simulations separated usable relay designs from ones whose individually stable stations still amplified errors along the route.)
- [39:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2395s) Hamming: Combine doubt with determination: "I can simulate anything to some degree" does not mean perfect answers. (context: He doubts both the supplied equations and his computed answers, yet considers the what-if question too important to abandon. At 42:04–42:35 he recalls obtaining partial answers to problems others gave up on. This is his working attitude and experience, not a guarantee that every problem is predictable or every approximation useful.)
- [40:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2426s) Hamming: Plan known special cases and internal checks before programming, and make them easy to run. (context: He asks about energy conservation and known solutions. Removing gravity and lift, he prescribes a constant turn and checks whether the computed circle closes. Loading gravity through one register makes zero-gravity tests safer than editing repeated 32.2 constants and risking missed occurrences; success on this special case is a check, not proof of the full model.)
- [44:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2650s) Hamming: Take responsibility for the problem statement, the computation, and the recipient's understanding. (context: His nightmare is having to pass a colleague in Bell Labs' long corridor after a faulty simulation caused that colleague to abandon an idea for which someone else won a Nobel Prize. To a physicist, he says, that prize is "something better than going to heaven". Whether the failure was wrong equations, wrong calculation, or misunderstood output, he would still have to face the colleague.)
- [46:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2777s) Hamming: Check what people actually hear, especially across rank differences; good intentions do not guarantee clear communication. (context: A vice president offered an employee leaving for another job permission to leave earlier than agreed. The employee heard that he was being fired for refusing to stay. Hamming uses this story to explain how expectations can distort a simulation report too; his claim that larger rank gaps increase misunderstanding is his observation, not a quantified law.)
- [47:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O5Ml5kPouG8&t=2841s) Hamming: Read a report for the ways someone could bend it toward the answer they already want. (context: The same curves may be read as reasons to approve or reject a project. He charges the simulator with making readers understand "what in Hell the damn thing is saying and is not saying". The duty includes stating the limits of the answer, whether the recipient outranks you or reports to you.)
