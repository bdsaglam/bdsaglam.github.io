# Lecture 2 - Foundations of the Digital (Discrete) Revolution · notes
- [01:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=93s) Hamming: Get reliable results by stopping errors from accumulating: digital repeaters "detect it and gate a new signal" instead of amplifying the noise too. Check an astonishing number with a small calculation. (context: His transcontinental telephone estimate is 3,000 miles, an amplifier every 50 miles, and gain 100 each: 60 stages give total gain 10^120. Digital regeneration and, when errors are not too numerous, error-correcting codes reduce the need for exquisitely accurate components.)
- [04:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=250s) Hamming: Connections can dominate a machine's cost and reliability: "solder joints were a real curse". (context: He describes acid residue causing intermittent failures, a problem integrated circuits largely removed. His approximate connection costs, from someone else's estimate, are 0.001 cent within a chip, 1 cent between chips, 10 cents between boards, and about a dollar between mainframes. These are period estimates, not current prices.)
- [06:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=373s) Hamming: Judge an information product by what it conveys, not its physical carrier: "the true content of a book or a film or such things is the information it contains". (context: He argues that work is shifting from making things to handling information. His forecast that fewer than 25% of workers would make anything by 2020 is explicitly a 1995 guess, not a measured result.)
- [07:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=435s) Hamming: Do not let a familiar machine shape limit what you imagine automating: "you have to take a reasonable view of what a computer could be". (context: A robot need not look human, and its controller could use neural networks, fuzzy logic, or parallel computation. His pill-making example favors machines for keeping a strong drug's dose consistent when people get bored or careless.)
- [09:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=564s) Hamming: Before mechanizing a job, "ask what are the fundamental things I'm trying to accomplish", then redesign an equivalent product. Trying to do what humans did in the same way "has produced any number of disasters". (context: Machine accounting could not rely on what Mrs. Smith silently knew; rivets and welding replaced screws and bolts. Organizational structure, too, may have to change to achieve the same underlying purpose.)
- [10:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=624s) Hamming: Plan for the work a new tool will make possible, rather than extending yesterday's facilities. His prediction reversed experiments from nine in ten in the lab to nine in ten on computers; management treated him as "a crazy theoretician". (context: Bell Labs fitted costly laboratories, then moved programmers into some before the doors were on. He traced his forecast to computing bomb designs at Los Alamos, where he says no small-scale critical-mass test was available. His later claim of roughly 99% computer experiments is his broad assertion, not a verified statistic.)
- [11:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=687s) Hamming: Use simulation to ask questions that the laboratory cannot isolate. He could remove the space-charge effect from a traveling-wave-tube calculation by taking out a term. (context: Beam charge caused spreading; removing its mathematical contribution let him explore a limiting case the experimenters could not physically switch off. He also valued having the numbers available during rapid changes that were hard to measure on the fly.)
- [12:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=747s) Hamming: Do not mistake a simulation for a fresh observation of nature: "we are going back to Medieval scholasticism". Ask "how far dare you go". (context: He contrasts Galileo's call to look at reality with reading Aristotle, then compares trusting computed predictions to trusting books. This warns about models' limits and discovering fundamentally new things, alongside his strong advocacy of simulation.)
- [14:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=847s) Hamming: When you must design before all the facts are known, leave room to revise the design: "computers give you an enormous flexibility". (context: Bomb engineering proceeded while experiments were still determining constants. Programmable control lets engineers update formulas and programs as the numbers become better known.)
- [16:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1003s) Hamming: "Leave the people at the bottom alone to make their mistakes and learn from those mistakes", where the damage is smaller. Micromanagement's greatest evil is failing to train successors. (context: Middle managers promoted after years of having decisions made for them have not learned to live with bad decisions. Front-line staff know local details better, but he also warns that local planners may miss the company's overall purpose: "both ways go wrong".)
- [18:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1126s) Hamming: Small specialists working together can defend against micromanagement while doing a large corporation's work. (context: Over hamburgers, a broker explained that outsourced bookkeeping and legal help let him concentrate on investments. Hamming's biotechnology investments likewise used firms that could develop a compound or sell it at successive stages. He says "I don't know how far that's going to go"; he also judges the AT&T breakup to have both good and bad effects, with more diverse services.)
- [20:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1222s) Hamming: Look beyond a technology's first paying customers, and consider the social limits on what it can do. (context: Expensive computers began with number crunching; lower prices and more storage opened word processing and airline reservations. He invites students to imagine chips and servos changing sex, family, sports, and virtual travel "in all the Comforts of home", while warning of legal and other restraints. These are possible futures he asks them to consider, not demonstrated outcomes.)
- [21:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1315s) Hamming: Possessing information is insufficient if you cannot act on it in time: "we had the information we didn't use it fast enough". (context: He attributes deaths in the Gulf War to failure to use available information, and describes the struggle to obtain information while denying it to the opponent. This is his account; he explicitly says he does not know how far it predicts future warfare.)
- [22:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1347s) Hamming: Account for human vulnerability when deciding which tasks to mechanize: "Battlefield is no place for a human being". (context: Quoting a sign, he contrasts caring for a wounded person with a damaged shipboard robot: "you can kick him overboard". The deliberately blunt example makes replacement and human care part of the comparison; he immediately acknowledges that machines also have disadvantages.)
- [24:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1444s) Hamming: Strip away local constants to see a model's underlying form: "get rid of the local constants". (context: Starting with growth proportional to current size, he adds a factor that slows growth near an upper limit, then changes variables to remove the scale constants before solving. The rescaled equation makes the common S-shaped pattern visible.)
- [28:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1691s) Hamming: Ask where growth will saturate before extrapolating it, and what new technology could start another curve: "typically it requires a new technology to get there". (context: He worried how long computing could keep doubling about every 18 months. Component size, signaling speed, and heat constrain a single processor; parallel machines suggest a new S-curve. He also considers logarithmic growth instead of a hard ceiling: these simple models give orientation, not precise forecasts.)
- [29:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1754s) Hamming: Build field maintenance into the first design; trying to "graft on reliability later on does not work out well". (context: As engineering becomes selecting chips and writing programs, he asks students to remember household devices that are thrown away because repair is too hard. Discarding a small radio or a badly damaged robot can be sensible, but valuable equipment needs maintenance planned from the start.)
- [30:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=1819s) Hamming: Beware a custom design chosen for prestige: a special-purpose chip means "a job so important we got a special chip for it". Count the future support and inventory costs too. (context: General-purpose chips bring other users' bug finding, manuals, and manufacturers' upgrades. Custom production leaves you deciding how many spares to stock, and a change of approach can make the whole inventory useless. He explicitly allows that special chips are sometimes necessary.)
- [33:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=2009s) Hamming: The rate of change is limited by people's ability to adapt: "you're going to be busy adjusting all the time". Respect habits because they free attention for useful work. (context: He noticed he put on his right sock first but his left shoe first, without thinking. Reorganizing a corporation every six months can produce chaos, and an unsuccessful trial may not let you return to the old organization; simulations help anticipate consequences without making the change.)
- [34:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=2070s) Hamming: Choose tools with a living user community, even over a temporarily better isolated tool: otherwise "you are down a dead end and you are there by yourself". (context: He preferred the computer with the biggest sales because users sometimes got ten times what manufacturers expected from early machines. Computing groups at competing airframe firms quietly traded programs and knowledge: "we couldn't cope with the machines without cooperation". This extends his warning about custom chips to the value of shared learning.)
- [37:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=2255s) Hamming: Keep analog methods available and combine them with digital decisions when that fits the job: "that combination of analog digital is very powerful". (context: A toilet float follows the water level and then snaps the valve shut; a thermostat combines a changing physical position with an on/off contact. He added switches to an inherited analog computer despite protests. His qualification is that analog methods struggle with deep computations or very high accuracy.)
- [39:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=2349s) Hamming: Choose components for reliable final answers, not purchase price alone. The enduring problem is "how to get reliable results out of unreliable Parts". (context: He recalls a commercial alternative with roughly one-part-in-1,000 accuracy at a tenth the cost, but would not give up that one binary digit per component. He reused gun-director parts rated around one part in 2,000, tested through Arctic and tropical conditions. He estimates one part in 10,000 would have cost 50–100 times as much; these are his equipment-specific recollections.)
- [41:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2i5w9onAsY&t=2475s) Hamming: "You must be responsible for your own beliefs." Work through a claim yourself rather than citing a professor or a famous name, and reconsider whether yesterday's advice fits tomorrow. (context: He gives what he presents as a translated saying of the Buddha, even questioning the teacher's own words; this is Hamming's attribution, not an independently checked quotation from the Buddha. He admits "my feet are in the past" and asks which of his claims will remain relevant when his students reach their imagined career peak in 2020.)
