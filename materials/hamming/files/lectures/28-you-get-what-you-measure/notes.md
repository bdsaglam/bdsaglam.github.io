# Lecture 28 - You Get What You Measure · notes
- [01:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=69s) Hamming: The instrument limits the conclusions you can draw: "the instrument they used affected what they got". (context: Fishermen examine their catch and infer a minimum fish size in the sea. A different mesh changes that apparent minimum; the net has selected the evidence, not established a limit in nature.)
- [01:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=101s) Hamming: Choose the time horizon of a measure deliberately; optimizing each quarter does not ensure the best long-run result. (context: Maximizing the quarterly bottom line discourages long-range planning. Hamming says short- and long-term goals can conflict, so progress must be judged with both in view.)
- [02:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=161s) Hamming: Promotion rules create the pool of future leaders: at a 95% starting rating, the strategy is "Don't Rock the Boat". (context: There is much to lose and little to gain, so cautious people rise; later there may be no risk taker available to rescue a failing organization. Starting everyone at 20% can encourage the opposite behavior, leaving only risk takers when steady operation is needed. These are illustrative incentive systems, not measured thresholds.)
- [04:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=256s) Hamming: Do not confuse a reliable measure with a relevant one. (context: Promoting by height would be accurate, reproducible, and easy to administer, yet say little about management ability. Morale is harder to measure but may matter more; convenience is not a reason to replace the property you need.)
- [06:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=416s) Hamming: A pattern built into calibration is not an independent discovery: "I made it that way by my calibration". (context: In his account of IQ testing, questions are selected for consistency and raw-score percentiles mapped onto a normal curve. Neither the resulting normal distribution nor a person's stable score alone proves that the test measures intelligence. This is his calibration argument and historical account, not a complete assessment of IQ validity.)
- [09:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=570s) Hamming: Design an evaluation around the decision it must support, and keep defensible evidence for consequential decisions. (context: Hamming says that, knowing a calculus class, he can shape its grade distribution through question difficulty. His final exam especially distinguishes who should fail, because failing a student is serious and the evidence may be challenged; the histogram alone does not reveal the class's ability.)
- [11:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=666s) Hamming: The range a rater uses silently changes the weight of that rater's opinion: "mine will dominate the rating". (context: With 5 as the neutral point, one person gives a favorable 6 while another gives an equally unfavorable opinion a 2. Blind averaging lets the wider-range rater dominate. The issue is inconsistent use of a shared scale, not necessarily a stronger underlying opinion.)
- [11:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=698s) Hamming: Treat grades as communication and ask how much distinction the scale actually conveys. (context: He invokes maximum entropy when all symbols are equally used, contrasting this with graduate grades restricted mainly to A and B. He also acknowledges that failing a student is serious; maximum symbol entropy alone does not establish the fairest or most valid grading policy.)
- [12:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=760s) Hamming: Ranking resists grade inflation but loses information about the strength of the comparison group: "one of them has to be the dummy". (context: Five very capable people can share a class, yet one must rank last. His Naval Academy example uses rank within a stated class size; bottom of that class does not by itself mean low absolute ability.)
- [13:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=825s) Hamming: Compare people across groups rather than trusting each group's isolated self-rating. (context: Bell Labs evaluated promotion and salary separately. Groups tended to rate their own people highly, so Hamming's computing contacts across departments made him useful in comparing people from different specialties; local enthusiasm did not supply a common standard.)
- [14:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=887s) Hamming: Disliking evaluation does not remove the need to learn how to do it: "since you're going to have to rate you might as well study". (context: Not everyone can head an organization; even an evening of skits or a lecture program needs a first and last performer. Hamming studied real rating sessions rather than treating his discomfort as an escape from judgment.)
- [15:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=949s) Hamming: Experts can agree on relative merit while disagreeing fiercely about the explanation. (context: Hank McDonald, a practical builder, and Hamming, a theoretician, agreed whether Joe was better than Pete but argued as soon as they were asked why. A skiing judge reported the same pattern. Hamming recommends relative judgments by mature, experienced evaluators; their agreement is evidence he trusts, not proof that intuition is infallible.)
- [18:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1107s) Hamming: Early selection can favor a different talent from the one a field needs at the top: a "sea of detail" can hide the bigger picture. (context: Calculus rewards error-free algebra and introductory computing rewards exact instructions, attracting what he calls "minutia people". Later, seeing whole structures matters. At 21:36 he describes mathematics as organizing whole ideas and the systems mathematician as the organization's conscience. His preceding caricature of psychology students as "mixed up" is his personal judgment, not established evidence about that field.)
- [20:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1200s) Hamming: Recruitment begins with the picture of a field that people see, and its effects last into leadership. (context: Bell Labs paid Hamming's travel so universities could hear exciting accounts of his work before recruiters arrived. He knew he was "a carrot being dangled"; he also admits most recruits would not have lives as exciting as his. He warns that repeatedly filling top positions from outside can discourage able insiders who see no path upward.)
- [22:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1328s) Hamming: Let people who recognize research originality help select researchers; conventional appearance can be the wrong filter. (context: Bell Labs sent senior researchers rather than personnel staff to recruit. Hamming argues that originality in science can accompany originality in dress or behavior, making some capable candidates unattractive to conventional recruiters. He recalls personnel rejecting a candidate who weighed about 400 pounds on medical grounds; others wanted him, while Hamming had separate objections. These are his anecdotes and generalizations, not a rule that eccentricity proves ability.)
- [24:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1454s) Hamming: Ask whether promotion selects future capability or merely people with whom current leaders feel comfortable. (context: Boards select successors who resemble themselves and their own earlier successes. Shared outlook gives coherence but can suppress innovation; hiring only troublemakers also obstructs decisions. He recalls university leaders intervening in departments dominated by one school of thought, while a homogeneous top leadership has nobody above it to force that correction.)
- [27:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1642s) Hamming: Independent members can improve decisions before they say anything, because insiders must consider how their behavior looks to outsiders. (context: On his board, outsiders brought different views and made insiders reconsider promotions and other actions. He wants compatibility together with independent opinion, rather than either continual squabbling or complete agreement.)
- [28:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1708s) Hamming: Audit whether outside advice changes anything, rather than treating repeated reviews as evidence of oversight. (context: On a government panel, Hamming asked for earlier reports and found the previous five panels had made the same major recommendation over roughly 10–15 years without action. He says organizers often cite recommendations they already favor and ignore the rest; this is his account of panel practice, not a verified finding about every review.)
- [29:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1770s) Hamming: Accept unfamiliar responsibilities deliberately when specialization is narrowing your life. (context: He first avoided a board invitation and obtained a legal decision against serving. Realizing his interests were becoming "narrower and narrower and narrower", he accepted a later invitation and obtained clearance. Board and outside-panel work became opportunities to learn how promotion, information, and management really function.)
- [32:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=1958s) Hamming: A consistent method can reveal subtle effects even when it is not demonstrably more accurate: "it's not that your answers are necessarily better". (context: For the phase-gain calculation, engineers approximated a gain curve with hand-drawn straight segments and tables. Television bandwidth made that impractical. His computer method removed variation between people's arbitrary segment choices, allowing finer effects to be investigated; he explicitly does not claim that automation alone made each answer better.)
- [34:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2083s) Hamming: Trust human judgment only with active self-scrutiny; apparent kindness can conceal prejudice. (context: As a young calculus instructor, he sometimes offered a disliked failing student a pass, then thought "I got my revenge" because the student would never properly learn the subject. He calls this evil and says he stopped. His preference for mature judgment requires evaluators to know and monitor their prejudices, not merely trust their feelings.)
- [36:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2177s) Hamming: Comparisons can create useful internal pressure even where external competition is absent. (context: AT&T compared operating companies through questionnaires, means, and variances, then investigated why morale was high in one company and low in another. In his account this produced internal competition within a monopoly; it illustrates a use for measurement alongside the lecture's warnings.)
- [36:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2209s) Hamming: A collection of successful inspections need not measure simultaneous readiness. (context: A skipper prepares successively for scheduled tests, so each report describes a different prepared moment. Hamming objects to feeding these figures into war games as the fleet's real readiness. Random inspections shorten preparation time but do not eliminate warning: radar and a grapevine supposedly "faster the velocity of light" can announce the inspectors. The speed phrase is his joke, not a physical claim.)
- [39:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2362s) Hamming: A nonlinear scale changes the distribution you see; do not mistake the chosen coordinates for nature's own units. (context: He contrasts feet-to-meters conversion with a logarithmic earthquake scale and sensory scales. The captions garble the scale's name and give an uncertain energy-conversion number, so that number is not retained as a fact. The point is to examine the transformation, not to reject logarithmic scales.)
- [40:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2424s) Hamming: Choose the scale for the decision: "different scales are needed for different things". (context: Adding 3 cows to 3 doubles a herd; adding 3 to 1,000 hardly changes it. Counts are useful for combining herds, while percentage changes may better express growth. Even in one field, a scale useful for one conclusion can be unsuitable for another.)
- [41:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2517s) Hamming: Reports can remain systematically misleading when everyone bends them in the same direction. (context: He says managers bend reports to look good and invokes the weak law of large numbers to suggest differently directed distortions may partly cancel. That is his heuristic, not a guarantee: common incentives can align distortions, defeating the hoped-for cancellation.)
- [43:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2610s) Hamming: A real shipment target can still damage the whole production system: meeting the quarter's number is not the same as improving output. (context: Salesmen were paid by the quarter, so unfinished machines were cannibalized to complete shipments before quarter-end. The following week or two then went into repairing the damage. Hamming found the shipments were real, but says even bringing the custom to top management did not stop it; an entrenched metric can create an entrenched habit.)
- [44:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2675s) Hamming: Verify selected reports against reality: prudence means you are "not a sucker for anything anybody ever tells you". (context: He came a day early or stayed a day late for board meetings, looked into the inventory crib, and checked the loading platform. His rough judgment that the goods might be worth $12 million was a plausibility check, not an independent audit. He presents these visits as evidence of care without maintaining a duplicate accounting system.)
- [45:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2740s) Hamming: Neither popularity nor management's demand for a number makes a measurement valid; preserve what the number actually means. (context: He describes a lower level reporting capability and the next level relabeling it as probability because the requested information cannot be supplied. At 46:11 he stresses that bending the meaning "didn't improve the numbers at all". His brief return to life testing reinforces the gap between what is wanted and what can be known.)
- [46:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2803s) Hamming: Before installing a rating system, ask "what will be the long-term Global effects" and who will occupy the positions ten years later. (context: People change their behavior to rate well; measurement affects whom the organization attracts, promotes, and eventually has available. This closing design question joins the earlier examples of risk-taking, recruitment, and promotion rather than assuming a metric passively records an unchanged organization.)
- [47:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LNhcaVi3zPA&t=2834s) Hamming: Rewarding lines of code penalizes cleanup and teaches habits that outlive the original measure. (context: He asks whether programmers paid in ratings for lines written have any incentive to clean up a program: "no"; any incentive to add "bells and whistles": "yes". At 47:44 he speculates that more than half of bloated software may be wasted. That is his estimate, not a measured fraction; his lesson concerns the incentive to preserve excess code and the difficulty of undoing the resulting style.)
