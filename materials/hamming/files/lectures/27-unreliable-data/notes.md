# Lecture 27 - Unreliable Data · notes
- [03:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=197s) Hamming: When testing highly reliable equipment, question the tester itself: "Something is very highly reliable. With what will you test?" (context: The Atlantic voice cable needed tubes to last 20 years, with each failure estimated to cost roughly a million dollars. The attic full of test equipment did not answer why the testers should be as reliable as the tubes.)
- [04:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=292s) Hamming: Frontier technology creates a conflict between long-life assurance and speed to deployment; "I don't have an answer." (context: He had less than 18 months to test tubes for a 20-year life. Heat, voltage, and frequency can accelerate failures, but have limits. The cable was eventually retired after 22 years because better systems made it obsolete; waiting for certainty has a cost too.)
- [05:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=356s) Hamming: Prepare to test the next invention before it arrives: "there's never enough time to do it right, but there's always time to fix it." (context: Bell Labs did not form his proposed forward-looking life-testing group because it was too busy testing current devices. He admits his suggestions for starting it were weak, and singles out software as familiar with the same trap.)
- [07:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=452s) Hamming: Good people and the "brass plate on the machine" do not establish actual accuracy; check by remeasurement and calibration. (context: His statistician friend claimed to have lost a briefcase, obtained new measurements, then produced the old data to expose disagreement. It hurt relations with the boss but saved trouble. Hamming's brother routinely took apart and recalibrated new air-pollution instruments.)
- [09:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=543s) Hamming: Automatic recording does not make a record correct: "The machine that placed the calls did not record correctly what the calls were." (context: A statistician found three-minute calls to nonexistent central offices, then discovered many such records. The same switching equipment could place real calls and misrecord their destinations.)
- [10:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=604s) Hamming: Check data consistency before calculating the answer, even when people press you to proceed. (context: Western Electric assured him its 18 months of inventory records had been cleaned so there were no withdrawals from empty inventory. He believed them, found more contradictions, and had to clean the records and repeat the entire simulation; later he insisted on checking first despite complaints.)
- [11:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=698s) Hamming: A pilot must test the people and process that will do the full job; a successful miniature can give false assurance. (context: The small AT&T card-punching pilot went to specialists, while the large study went to a different group. They had not understood the pilot's purpose, and Hamming admits he had failed to anticipate how the organization would route the work.)
- [12:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=759s) Hamming: Compare old uncertainty claims with later independent results, rather than accepting prestigious measurements on authority. (context: Taking newer physical constants as correct, he found the earlier values differed by over five times their stated probable errors on average; another comparison gave roughly two and a half. These are his historical comparisons, conditional on the later values, not proof that every scientific error bar is wrong.)
- [14:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=853s) Hamming: "Reproducibility does not mean the right answer." Tuning for low variance can conceal error instead of removing it. (context: An experimenter fine-tunes equipment until repeated runs agree, then gives those measurements to a statistician. The small spread reflects what the apparatus was tuned to produce; Hamming offers this as part of the explanation for overstated accuracy.)
- [15:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=917s) Hamming: His warning about overstated accuracy is: "90% of the time the next independent measurement will be outside the 90% confidence limits not inside." (context: He immediately calls this an exaggeration, suggests 85 instead, and says "90 sounds much better." It is his experience-based warning, not a measured coverage law. His example is Hubble-constant measurements lying outside one another's reported errors.)
- [16:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=978s) Hamming: As measurement improves, recheck the model: "People don't come with negative heights." (context: A normal model of human height allows impossible negative values. Model error may be hidden by noisy measurements at first, then loom larger as the instruments improve while attention remains on the equipment.)
- [17:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1070s) Hamming: Do not let a precise component lend false precision to a whole decision: "a chain is as strong as the weakest link." (context: A computer company combined careful engineering cost estimates with a salesman's intuitive estimates of sales at different prices, then treated the result as having the engineers' reliability. Hamming explicitly says he is not claiming the salesman was wrong; the neglected issue was uncertainty.)
- [18:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1135s) Hamming: Cross-check official accounts of the same event: "I can't see how gold can change from gold." (context: Citing Morgenstern, he says British reports of gold shipped to France and French reports of gold received sometimes differed two to one. He can imagine electronics being relabeled as medical gear to avoid duties, but gold makes the mismatch striking.)
- [21:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1292s) Hamming: Before interpreting a long trend, ask whether the measured thing or its definition has changed, even without an official announcement. (context: Inventory reports changed with tax rules, so a change need not express expected sales. Wheat varieties and naval jobs also changed under familiar names. His provocative poverty example makes the same point about moving thresholds; his claim that poverty therefore cannot be removed is his assertion, not a conclusion established by these examples.)
- [23:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1389s) Hamming: Imperfect historical data can still be useful: use signal analysis to investigate trends while remembering the changing definitions. (context: He suggests comparing capital investment with other assets and employee numbers, and using digital filters to reduce background noise. The qualification matters: his warning about unreliable data is not an instruction to abandon analysis, and filtering does not repair a changed definition.)
- [25:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1513s) Hamming: An unchanged index can preserve comparability while losing relevance; "Institutions like people hate to change." (context: He argues that economic indices lagged the shift from manufacturing toward information work. Their defenders resisted updating them because the old and new series would not be comparable. His criticism is of preserving the series while letting its connection to the economy weaken.)
- [26:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1575s) Hamming: Look for changing hidden biases, not just a fixed offset you could correct once. (context: He describes secret discounts to favored customers that change with prosperity and depression. An indicator based on visible prices can then miss what money actually changes hands; "But it's a changing difference.")
- [27:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1637s) Hamming: Ask what the data was collected for and what its providers gain by reporting particular numbers. (context: Citing Morgenstern, he recounts countries seeking Marshall Plan aid saying they would report figures they thought would obtain the most aid. Such strategically reported numbers can later become official historical data.)
- [28:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1705s) Hamming: With the same budget, careful sampling can beat complete but careless collection: "it's better to take a small sample accurately done than a big sample poorly done." (context: He contrasts a carefully done 1% or 0.1% survey with a 100% survey using the same money. Telephone companies and airlines allocated revenue from small, carefully drawn samples. His comparison depends on collection quality and resources; it does not mean smaller samples are inherently more accurate.)
- [30:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1829s) Hamming: Get expertise in asking questions, not only in computing statistics: small wording changes can change the answer. (context: Question order and the interviewer also affect responses. Two pollsters can appear to ask the same thing yet obtain different answers; he urges hiring someone who understands polls and can seek unbiased questions, since the same skills can deliberately produce a desired answer.)
- [31:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1861s) Hamming: Before trusting a social statistic, imagine how anyone could actually obtain it honestly. (context: At dinner a widow challenged his skepticism, and he asked how to estimate adultery per year on the Monterey Peninsula: would she trust questionnaires or follow people? Novels and one's own circle suggest different impressions; he admits "I haven't any idea." The lesson is a measurement obstacle, not an estimate of prevalence.)
- [32:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=1954s) Hamming: An average can hide the groups you need to understand: "the average adult has one bosom and one testicle." (context: After filling out an important questionnaire honestly, he realized it mixed different groups. At 33:05 he says averaging is for "somewhat homogeneous" data and adds the family with 2.6 children. The warning concerns treating a pooled average as representative of subgroups or a real individual; it is not a universal mathematical ban on pooled averages.)
- [34:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=2048s) Hamming: Learn how reports are produced before relying on their totals; fabricated answers need not bias them in a predictable direction. (context: He says groups expecting good ratings respond promptly; others delay until a junior person fills the questionnaires as best they can at the deadline. He does not know whether these answers raise, lower, or leave the result unchanged. A leader must improve the reporting process rather than assume the errors cancel.)
- [35:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-0kk-qDpuI&t=2109s) Hamming: Observe while you are junior, because rank later changes what people show you: "Your very presence will screw it." (context: As a company director he saw people do what they thought would please him, although it annoyed him. An admiral likewise changes behavior merely by appearing. Direct observation then becomes harder just as responsibility grows; "Guns are easy to count but attitudes are harder.")
