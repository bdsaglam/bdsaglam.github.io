# Lecture 4 - History of Computers - Software · notes
- [02:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=162s) Hamming: Separate a useful idea from the person who gets credit for reporting it. He calls attribution of stored programming to von Neumann "probably very dubious". (context: He cites Harry Huskey’s account of discussions before von Neumann became a consultant; this is Hamming’s historical judgment, not a settled priority finding.)
- [03:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=224s) Hamming: Local patches can create a global mess; change the representation when the old one blocks growth. Control became "like a can of spaghetti". (context: Absolute-address fixes jumped to spare memory and back; fixed-location library routines then crowded memory, motivating relocatable software.)
- [06:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=382s) Hamming: Include human labor when deciding what is efficient. After a year of absolute-binary programming, he vowed, "I will never again ask any human being to do a job like that". (context: A woman did most of the work filling the IBM 701’s 32,000 registers. Machine rental was $300 an hour; his inflation-adjusted comparison of almost a dollar a second explains why people had prioritized machine time.)
- [07:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=475s) Hamming: Expert pride can delay useful tools: "programmers were very very slow in adopting the better methods". (context: Of roughly 100 IBM 701 users, he recalls only one interested in symbolic programming. FORTRAN met three objections: impossible, too inefficient, and "nobody but sissies" would use it. These are his recollections of resistance, not adoption statistics.)
- [09:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=569s) Hamming: Design a language around its users: "look at the people who are going to use it" and "ask how they think". (context: FORTRAN translated familiar school mathematics; he contrasts its survival with ALGOL’s logical elegance and decline. This is his argument for psychological design, not proof that logical rigor and usability cannot coexist.)
- [09:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=599s) Hamming: A tool specialized for one domain still needs to work with other domains. (context: Enthusiasm for problem-oriented languages faded when one problem needed two specialties and their languages would not blend; he allows that business and scientific languages still have uses.)
- [11:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=661s) Hamming: A mandated tool may produce nominal compliance and extra errors if users cannot work naturally in it. (context: Friends running software houses told him "about 90% of the time" programs would be written and debugged in FORTRAN, then converted to Ada. He presents this as an informal inquiry and suspicion about practice, not a measured industry rate.)
- [13:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=788s) Hamming: Make tools do work on themselves: "make a lot of work be done by the work you’re doing". (context: SOAP optimized its own instruction placement on the IBM 650 drum; timing the same input under the original and optimized assembler exposed the gain. He links this to the preceding Lisp self-assembly story as a reusable recursive technique.)
- [14:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=883s) Hamming: Doing something once is different from recognizing its general meaning. He asks, "when is something known"? (context: A translation program remained in Appendix D of the EDSAC book; he doubts its authors recognized the general idea. Audiences also nodded at computers as symbol manipulators without, he thought, grasping the change. His doubts about the authors and Turing are interpretations, not established facts about their understanding.)
- [18:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1101s) Hamming: A language’s meaning comes from what its operations actually do: "all meaning is contained in what those sub routines are". (context: His IBM 650 interpreter fetched an instruction, separated its addresses, and dispatched to the corresponding routine. Changing those routines changed the apparent machine; flags could make later behavior depend on earlier instructions.)
- [18:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1131s) Hamming: Choose translation layers for the job: an interpreter repeats the work on each visit, while a compiler makes the translation "once for all". (context: He uses floating-point software on the fixed-point 650 to explain why runtime interpretation can be useful, then describes stacking compilers and interpreters. His halting floating-point explanation is specific to that implementation, not a general claim that floating point cannot be compiled.)
- [20:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1223s) Hamming: Build protection into what a program can express. (context: He placed his interpreter in the upper 1,000 of 2,000 registers and gave user instructions three-digit addresses, so they could reach only 000–999. The caption’s lower 10,000 is inconsistent with those limits; the chapter confirms the lower 1,000, keeping user programs from overwriting the interpreter.)
- [20:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1255s) Hamming: An inventor’s authority need not settle how a field should develop; when you are the inventor, "remember to shut up". (context: Told that he did not understand error-correcting codes, he answered "yes he is probably right". He argues that followers can see beyond the difficulties that shaped a founder’s thinking; Newton as "the last of the Ancients" is his parallel, not a universal law about creators.)
- [22:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1348s) Hamming: Test the crucial low-level assumption before committing to a high-level design. (context: He began with ease of learning, use, debugging, and subroutine use, but first wrote the interpreter’s inner loop to check speed. Only then did he resume top-down design; a preferred method did not remove the need for bottom-up work.)
- [23:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1412s) Hamming: Learning a tool changes the questions you can ask with it. His target was a language "so easy that the physicists and chemists and Engineers could not refuse to learn it". (context: Early users brought calculations they would have done without computers, only faster. He wanted domain experts to learn enough computing to formulate different problems, not merely send old work to programmers.)
- [25:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1506s) Hamming: Human-facing languages need enough redundancy to expose mistakes, without making everything tedious to write. (context: A tiny APL change could change the whole meaning. He estimates speech at 60% redundancy and writing at 40%, explaining that speech must be caught on a noisy channel while text permits backtracking; these are his illustrative estimates, not universal measured constants.)
- [26:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1600s) Hamming: Study what features of a language accomplish, not only how they are arranged. He asks about "the engineering efficiency of language". (context: At lunch Professor Pei could describe where languages put plurals, but, in Hamming’s account, could not answer how synonyms, irregular verbs, and redundancy help communication between fallible humans and machines.)
- [27:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1664s) Hamming: The literal words may not carry the real message. His wife’s weather remark meant, to him, "I’ve had my second cup of coffee and I’m fit to talk to". (context: Both could see the rain through their breakfast-room window. He spent the day noticing how much conversation did something other than state facts; a human–computer language need not reproduce every social function of conversation.)
- [29:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1758s) Hamming: Design input and output for their different readers: "they need not be the same language". (context: He wanted terse input but more explanatory machine output, since cryptic output can be unreadable. He connects the larger communication problem to what he calls the Japanese fifth-generation project’s software failure; that verdict and his claim to have predicted it are his assessments.)
- [30:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1851s) Hamming: Do not assume software production will behave like physical engineering: programming is, in his analogy, "closer to novel writing". (context: Space programs face the same physical laws, whereas two novelists given the same theme—or two programmers a complex problem—can produce very different works. He explicitly calls this an intellectual experiment, not a controlled comparison or proof that software cannot be engineered.)
- [32:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=1943s) Hamming: "Think before you write"; decide "what your acceptance test will be" before coding, so testability shapes a simpler design. (context: A colleague wandered the halls twisting his hair for three or four days, then wrote and debugged thousands of lines, producing more per week than immediate typists. Hamming immediately allows an exception when programming is needed to discover the problem.)
- [34:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2070s) Hamming: On an open-ended problem, "sometimes programming is a means for finding out what you’re trying to do"; once you understand it, start the program again. (context: The Nike guided-missile contract could not specify reasonable ranges and other details before exploration. He warns that fixing such a contract prematurely gives bad results, and recommends discarding exploratory code after it has taught you the problem.)
- [35:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2139s) Hamming: Software productivity remains a human bottleneck even as hardware speeds up. (context: He cites someone’s combined estimate of roughly 90-fold improvement, rounds it to 100, and compares four months’ work with one day. He contrasts this rough historical estimate with machines reaching a billion operations per second and warns that parallel machines add programming burdens.)
- [36:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2202s) Hamming: Judge programmers by what they deliver, not as interchangeable headcount. He says productivity differs "more than 10 to one" and suggests paying a poor programmer "to stay home and not come to work". (context: His provocative staffing advice follows conversations with software-house owners about paying reliable producers well. The factor and pay-to-stay-home conclusion are his claims, without a study or measurement method supplied here.)
- [38:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2295s) Hamming: Experience and a list of languages do not by themselves establish quality; develop criteria and criticize your own work against them. "Good writing is like playing a good game of tennis". (context: He asks whether a bureaucrat’s prose improves after twenty years, then invokes E. B. White failing his own stylistic advice: knowing the moves does not ensure you can put them together. This is his analogy and tentative explanation, not evidence that experience never helps.)
- [39:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2357s) Hamming: Do not turn a preferred method into a universal test of ability. (context: Two programmers he considered among Bell Labs’ best described opposite approaches: sketching the whole structure versus expanding outward from a block. His own superb programmer could work bottom-up but not top-down; he favors top-down while admitting the evidence is not strong enough to call it always best.)
- [40:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2451s) Hamming: Learn style by comparing concrete bad and good programs; a textbook example is not automatically a good model. (context: He pressed Kernighan to write such a book and supplied programming textbooks. He recalls their supposed models becoming bad examples, including a long program reduced to about four lines; some, he says, did not even compute their intended answer.)
- [42:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2545s) Hamming: Self-imposed conventions reduce errors and make code recognizable: "the adoption of rigid rules for myself was a great help". (context: He always wrote loop counters the same way and printed inputs immediately, so an answer retained the parameters defining its problem. His loop convention could not execute zero times—a bad fit for some AI work—so consistency helped without making his chosen convention universally right.)
- [44:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2641s) Hamming: Do not compete with the people whose work you manage. His boss told him, "you cannot compete with the people who working for you". (context: Hamming protested being told to stop programming, then agreed it was unfair to his staff. After moving somewhere without programmers reporting to him, he could write programs and a calculator-programming manual again.)
- [45:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2735s) Hamming: Team conventions must be checkable by the machine. If you cannot write a checker, "you haven’t got the manpower to check that everybody is following the rules". (context: Shared structures let readers recognize blocks and orient themselves quickly. He distinguishes team work from individual style and acknowledges having very little team-programming experience.)
- [47:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2829s) Hamming: Never skip testing because a change looks too small to matter: "it crashed the whole damn East Coast practically". (context: In his account, a telephone-code speedup bypassed the usual three-month testing routine because it seemed obviously equivalent. The outage’s extent and cause are his account here; even rearranging instructions can have unexpected consequences.)
- [47:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2859s) Hamming: Write so your future self can understand it, and avoid dependencies on peculiar hardware. "Don’t do some cute trick you won’t remember". (context: He chose the eleventh IBM 650 off the line to avoid early machines with unusual fixes, and ordered minimal attachments so his programs could run elsewhere. His aim was portability across ordinary machines, not merely success on his own.)
- [49:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2e5_Z6oZ0rM&t=2952s) Hamming: Treat programming as a central problem of human effort: "humans are going to become more expensive machines are going to become cheaper". (context: He closes by connecting software’s growing cost to understanding how people think, leading into applications and AI; this is his forecast from 1995.)
