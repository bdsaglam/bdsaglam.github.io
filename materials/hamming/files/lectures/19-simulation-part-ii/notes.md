# Lecture 19 - Simulation, Part II · notes
- [01:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=61s) Hamming: Before computing, ask both whether the calculation is correct and whether the model represents the situation adequately. More computation cannot rescue a wrong model. (context: He opens by rejecting team size and machine size as evidence of relevance. He urges answering the relevance question before starting, while allowing that sometimes work must begin before the answer is clear.)
- [02:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=156s) Hamming: Make sure the decision maker understands what a reliability number actually measures: "does the man in Washington who signs off that you can do the flight know this"? (context: At a Pasadena dinner, a speaker claimed something like 99.44% reliability after only about nine space shots, despite failures Hamming recalled. Pressed, the speaker distinguished simulation from reality but would not say whether the approver understood. Hamming inferred misunderstanding; this is his recollection, not a verified launch history.)
- [04:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=279s) Hamming: A successful trainer must change when the real equipment changes, or it may be "training the pilot possibly to fail". (context: Flight simulators safely expose pilots to dangerous emergencies and a range of experience unavailable in real flying. Their usefulness makes it more important to capture the new interactions of each new airplane, rather than carry yesterday's model forward.)
- [07:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=463s) Hamming: Match validation effort to the purpose, but do not claim accuracy you never checked. (context: For the 1955 Bell Labs open house, he replaced classified missile trajectories with a two-dial tennis game: racket angle and hitting strength, no spin. One tennis-playing physicist tuned it; another independently found it plausible. Hamming skipped fan tests for drag, drop tests for bounce, and filmed-trajectory comparisons because a demonstration did not justify the expense. Plausibility was enough for that purpose, not proof of accuracy.)
- [09:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=557s) Hamming: Do not mistake unfamiliarity for stupidity: "they are ignorant but not stupid". Adapt explanations to the audience, and remember that one day you will be the senior person struggling with a new idea. (context: He reports that no older visitor grasped his tennis game while almost every child did. A Naval War College conversation supplied the phrase. His broad claim about older minds is his interpretation of these experiences, not a controlled finding about age.)
- [10:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=651s) Hamming: Ask for governing relationships and real observations before agreeing to simulate a field. (context: Asked to model ecology, he wanted to know how much growth came from water and sunshine, and whether he could reproduce an observed ten-year forest history. Without such information, he declined; the requesters looked elsewhere. The ability to run a model was not his criterion for accepting the job.)
- [11:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=682s) Hamming: Check whether a dramatic prediction comes from evidence or is already built into the model. (context: He attacks the Club of Rome forecast of collapse around 2020, claiming its formulas forced that outcome and its calculations were inaccurate. These are Hamming's allegations in the lecture, not independently established findings here; the lesson is to examine the mechanism behind a headline result.)
- [12:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=745s) Hamming: Do not give an empirical model the confidence of a mathematical identity. His deliberately austere "Hamming's law of Economics" is "what is not produced cannot be consumed". (context: Wheat not raised cannot be eaten. He contrasts this constraint with economic rules he had seen fail, including the expectation that unemployment and inflation would not coexist. His sweeping distrust of other economic laws is his judgment, not a demonstration that all economic modeling is useless.)
- [15:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=905s) Hamming: Aggregation can create a pattern absent within the groups; inspect the mixture and the assumptions behind it. (context: His Berkeley admissions story uses explicitly made-up numbers: 100 men and 100 women apply, with 70 men and 30 women accepted. In his account, application mixes differ across departments with different acceptance rates, illustrating Simpson's paradox. At 23:27–23:58 he identifies the hidden assumption of uniform applications. His account does not establish the actual admissions figures or prove his explanation of earlier educational discrimination.)
- [16:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=968s) Hamming: Simplification is defensible when you know when the omitted details matter. (context: He could represent a spacecraft as a stick because mechanics supplied the limits. During a midcourse correction, people had to stay still: their motion could rotate the cabin by conservation of angular momentum and spoil the carefully aimed thrust. This is a reasoned simplification, not a general license to combine everything into one variable.)
- [19:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1153s) Hamming: A prediction can change the behavior it predicts; ask who can react to the output and undo its assumptions. (context: His insurance analogy reverses the bets for life insurance and annuities; insurers guard against behavior that defeats their tables. A widely known profitable trading rule invites reactions that erode it, while false inside information can induce trades benefiting the rumor's source. His claims about pervasive insider dealing are his assessment, not verified evidence here.)
- [19:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1185s) Hamming: When precise individual prediction is unjustified, offer scenarios of what to expect rather than pretend to know the outcome. (context: Spock's baby book describes typical ages for walking, talking, and saying no, not exactly when your baby will do each. Hamming says it emphasizes relatively stable biology over changing culture. He presents this course similarly: possible patterns for the future, not a detailed forecast of it.)
- [21:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1281s) Hamming: Use domain expertise and several kinds of checks; a small omitted effect or inaccurate input can defeat a polished simulation. (context: Ask whether the laws are well supported, whether an expert sees a missing effect, whether initial data are accurate enough, and whether known cases can be reproduced. Conservation of energy and mass provide internal checks. Hamming still warns that some risk remains; no single check establishes relevance.)
- [22:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1375s) Hamming: Verify that the equations actually became the intended program. A simulator can teach someone to "play the right game the wrong way". (context: Programs used for years can still contain serious bugs. In war gaming and business training, the damage is not just a wrong printed answer: people may practice habits that fail when they act outside the simulation.)
- [25:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1532s) Hamming: Automate the bookkeeping so experts can concentrate on the judgment only they can supply. (context: For upper-atmosphere chemistry after a nuclear explosion, roughly 100 possible interactions led to a large equation system. Chemists selected reaction cards; a program generated the equations and code. A reaction's loss in one state had to balance its gain in another, with matching coefficients and opposite signs. This reduced transcription errors while leaving the chemistry decisions with the chemists.)
- [27:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1628s) Hamming: "With the power of decision rests the responsibility": someone else's simulation does not remove your duty to judge its relevance. (context: You cannot inspect every detail, but you can ask for runs of cases whose outcomes you know. He also warns against casually dismissing simulations: they can handle complications you cannot think through yourself. There is no simple test or magic remedy that decides whether to trust one.)
- [28:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1722s) Hamming: Choose the numerical sampling rate from the dynamics you must preserve, rather than assuming continuous analog computation is inherently superior. (context: He contrasts Nyquist reconstruction using samples over the entire time axis with practical differential-equation integration, for which he reports needing seven to ten samples per cycle of the highest frequency. Engineers helped identify that frequency. This is his practical rule for typical integration formulas, not a universal bound; his finite-energy argument motivates an effective bandwidth, not proof of a strict frequency cutoff.)
- [30:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1826s) Hamming: Compare accumulated precision as well as speed: a "Deep" computation repeatedly uses earlier results, letting analog component errors propagate. (context: He recalls analog components accurate to one part in 2,000, suggests one part in 5,000 might then be available at a price, and contrasts this with digital computation carrying ten or more decimal places. These are his period-specific estimates; the judgment is to examine error through the whole chain, not just one component.)
- [31:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1888s) Hamming: Keep useful tools available even when they have fallen out of fashion. (context: He thinks the neglect of analog computation is a mistake: it runs in parallel, though scaling a problem, especially time, is difficult. He points to neural networks, analog or digital, as ways to find patterns that would be hard to program explicitly. His endorsement is alongside the precision limits he has just described.)
- [32:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=1951s) Hamming: Separate obtaining an answer from understanding enough to act on it: "would I dare to operate in medicine if I don't understand why the neural net said" to do so? (context: He is wary, yet explicitly willing to explore answers without full understanding. He contrasts medical action with his report that Tokyo subway neural-network controls ride more smoothly, stop more accurately, and use less power than human operators. That performance story is his claim, not an independently checked benchmark here.)
- [34:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=2075s) Hamming: Ask how small errors grow in the system before judging how predictable it is. (context: He contrasts the bomb simulation discussed in the preceding lecture with weather prediction: small deviations mattered little in one setting and grew large in the other. Feedback and stability limit what a simulation can usefully tell you, even when it captures many details.)
- [36:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=2170s) Hamming: Simulation becomes necessary when a real experiment cannot be undone, but that necessity does not make the model reliable. (context: Wondering what life would have been like had you married the other person has no repeatable experiment. Reorganizing a corporation has the same difficulty: restoring the old chart cannot erase people's memory of the disruption. Human reactions make such management simulations both desirable and hard to validate.)
- [37:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkrynGWfJng&t=2264s) Hamming: Trust intuition when the experience behind it still fits: "when your past experiences do not fit the situation then your gut feelings are not so good". (context: When model and expert disagree, he favors the expert if technology changes slowly; with rapid technological change, he discounts the expert's inherited intuition. He first says he bets on the simulation, then immediately corrects himself to the expert in the slow-change case. Intuition compresses experience; it does not guarantee transfer to a new setting.)
