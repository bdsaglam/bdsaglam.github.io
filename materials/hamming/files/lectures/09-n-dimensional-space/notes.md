# Lecture 9 - n-Dimensional Space · notes
- [00:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=41s) Hamming: A prepared mind gets more from an event by asking questions beyond its announced subject: "you see more than other people did". (context: After the previous class discussion, he asks what its resistance to machines reveals about introducing them at work, and where one might look for future leaders among participants and nonparticipants. These are questions and probabilistic clues, not rules that quiet students cannot lead; he explicitly says some back-row students are the smartest.)
- [02:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=167s) Hamming: Step back from daily work to see the pattern: "now that I was detached from the day-to-day activities", he saw the same kind of trouble in project after project. It "did not happen in an afternoon". (context: When he came to teach after 30 years at Bell Labs, he put his feet on the desk for many hours and asked what he had learned. The repeated pattern was that all design happens in n-dimensional space.)
- [03:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=198s) Hamming: "You build three-dimensional things. The design space is n dimensional." Count adjustable parameters when judging how hard a search will be. (context: His Bell Labs projects built physical objects, but each parameter added a dimension to their designs. He makes the vastness memorable with fish that school, follow the surface or bottom, or gather at a river mouth to find mates rather than wander the open ocean; this is his analogy, not a demonstrated biological explanation.)
- [06:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=384s) Hamming: Make a hard expression manageable by changing its form and recalling what the new form resembles. (context: For factorials, products are awkward, so he takes logarithms to get a sum; sums suggest integrals, and integration by parts removes the awkward logarithm. His derivation models how to choose a next move from the structure of the problem rather than simply recall a finished formula.)
- [10:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=605s) Hamming: Ask which kind of error an approximation controls: a ratio approaching one can coexist with a growing absolute difference. (context: Stirling's approximation improves in ratio while its numerical error grows. He illustrates this with x + sqrt(x) versus x: the difference sqrt(x) grows without bound, but their ratio tends to one.)
- [13:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=831s) Hamming: Notice when a trick can become a reusable method. (context: To evaluate the Gaussian integral, he multiplies two copies and changes to polar coordinates, where the sum of squares becomes a radius squared. He then reuses the same idea with n copies to find sphere volumes; the caption garbles the wording and attribution of his saying about a trick used twice, so this is a paraphrase.)
- [18:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1093s) Hamming: Do not assume that adding dimensions makes a fixed-radius neighborhood contain more volume. (context: He finds that unit-ball volume first rises, then falls toward zero because factorial growth beats a fixed exponential. Even a large radius must be held fixed for this limiting claim; it does not say volume shrinks if the radius is allowed to grow arbitrarily with dimension.)
- [19:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1154s) Hamming: "Almost all the volume is on the surface" means near the boundary, not literally on it. (context: His shell fraction is 1 - (1 - epsilon)^n. For any fixed relative thickness epsilon between zero and one, it approaches one as dimension grows. Even in three dimensions, the outer half-radius shell contains 7/8 of the volume; "there's nothing inside" is his emphatic shorthand for concentration, not an empty interior.)
- [20:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1246s) Hamming: Look explicitly at the limits of the feasible region: a good design often pushes "one or more of the parameters to the extreme". (context: He connects the thin-shell calculation to missiles and airplanes and warns against relying only on interior stationary points from calculus. His stronger claim that the optimum is almost certainly on the surface is an engineering heuristic here; volume concentration alone does not prove where an arbitrary objective is optimized or make calculus useless.)
- [22:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1338s) Hamming: Blind local search can miss the best design: "you need inspiration of where to look". (context: He says North Jersey's lowest point is at the bottom of some mine shaft; wandering the surface will not tell you where to descend. In design, good regions can be separated by mountains, so a local optimum need not be the global best.)
- [23:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1403s) Hamming: "If you believe what the manufacturer says, you deserve what you get." Be able to look through their work yourself and ask whether a better design was possible. (context: Many students will one day be responsible for accepting a new design, picked from a vast n-dimensional space.)
- [24:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1499s) Hamming: A direction can be almost perpendicular to every coordinate axis, so familiar axis pictures can hide what is happening across many parameters at once. (context: The vector (1, 1, ..., 1) has length sqrt(n), but its component on each axis is only 1. Its angle with each axis approaches 90 degrees because the cosine is 1/sqrt(n).)
- [26:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1593s) Hamming: Distinguish exact perpendicularity from near perpendicularity when reasoning about real systems with small errors. (context: For random corner vectors with independent coordinates +1 or -1, the cosine is an average of random signs and is likely to be near zero in high dimensions. His contrast with just n perpendicular axes concerns approximate directions: it does not make all 2^n corner directions mutually near-perpendicular or invalidate representation in an n-vector basis.)
- [29:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1787s) Hamming: When a high-dimensional result contradicts your picture, inspect the reasoning: "Your last chance to complain." (context: Unit spheres centered at all combinations of +1 and -1 fit in the cube from -2 to 2 in each coordinate. The central sphere tangent to them has radius sqrt(n) - 1; at n = 10 it exceeds 2 and reaches outside the cube despite touching all 1,024 corner spheres on their inner sides. A three-dimensional drawing cannot settle the objection.)
- [32:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=1945s) Hamming: Do not assume a few sampled trajectories safely bound everything between them in a many-variable system. (context: After the sphere paradox, he recalls a Navy pursuit problem with 28 variables and equations: paths between tested initial conditions might reach farther than the surrounding paths suggest. This is his warning about an unsafe picture of trajectories as local tubes, not a proof that every interpolated trajectory escapes.)
- [36:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2166s) Hamming: Choose what distance means from the problem before using familiar geometry or fitting tools. (context: Manhattan streets require accumulated coordinate differences, L1, rather than a straight-line L2 distance; other design requirements care about the single largest absolute deviation, L-infinity. He warns that a real design can mix these criteria, making the search more complicated than his Euclidean examples.)
- [38:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2288s) Hamming: A comparison may fail because of one decisive mismatch or because too many small differences accumulate. (context: "Is that your daughter? No, it's too tall" illustrates L-infinity; a child who differs a little in many traits illustrates L1. He also counts differing bits with L1 and jokes about being "Pythagoras Junior". His broad preference for L1 or L-infinity in intellectual judgments and claims about AI practice are his 1995 perspective, not a universal prescription against L2.)
- [41:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2473s) Hamming: "It is better to get the right problem solved a little bit slowly than to rapidly solve the wrong problem." Do not choose a familiar mathematical model merely because you can solve it elegantly. (context: Less familiar distance criteria can be harder to calculate, but he would use computing power on the appropriate criterion and accept an approximate answer to the right problem. He recalls building filters that answered the wrong problem exactly.)
- [42:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2538s) Hamming: "The greatest step in creativity is recognizing that there is a problem. The second greatest step is identifying the nature of the problem." (context: Organizations contain problems nobody will acknowledge. He attributes to Pauli the view that a well-defined problem is no longer of much interest to a physicist: the difficult creative work often comes before applying mathematical techniques.)
- [43:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2635s) Hamming: You cannot work on an ill-defined problem, so you "define families of problems" and start working on those. (context: Systems engineering says there is no definite problem, and he agrees there isn't one. You still need a definite problem before you can do anything.)
- [44:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2697s) Hamming: Count the combinations before planning an exhaustive search; faster machines do not remove the growth caused by adding parameters. (context: Just ten values for each of ten variables already give 10^10 candidate designs. He returns to the need to search a vast feasible region rather than inspect every possibility.)
- [46:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2760s) Hamming: Do not assume changing one design parameter leaves the others alone; follow the consequences around the system until they settle. (context: A more powerful, heavier airplane motor requires stronger wings and body; the added weight requires larger wings, which need strengthening again. Even a small change can trigger a chain of coupled adjustments.)
- [46:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2791s) Hamming: Include tolerance to variation in what you call a good design: ideal conditions "are not realized. No way." (context: A central office designed exactly for maximum traffic collapses just above it; a bridge supporting exactly ten tons breaks when someone adds "a pound of candy in his pocket". These deliberately sharp examples argue for margin and robustness, not that every optimized design must be fragile.)
- [48:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2883s) Hamming: Ask what a successful test actually establishes: "One drone once." (context: His Nike missile example shoots down a drone with advance knowledge of roughly when, where, and at what altitude it will arrive. He asks how good that test is, then notes that shooting down 10,000 drones is unaffordable; a staged success alone does not demonstrate reliability across operating conditions.)
- [48:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uU_Q2a0S0zI&t=2913s) Hamming: "We do not test the way you think we do by repeated trials." When lifetime trials are impossible, reliability also has to be judged from the design. (context: In his undersea-cable account, vacuum tubes had to last 20 years, each failure would cost a million dollars, and only about one year was available before commitment. His sharper demand is the latest technology in the field tomorrow, lasting 20 years: the schedule cannot supply that lifetime evidence. These costs and schedules are his historical account.)
