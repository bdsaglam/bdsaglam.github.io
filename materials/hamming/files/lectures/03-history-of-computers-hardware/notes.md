# Lecture 3 - History of Computers - Hardware · notes
- [01:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=98s) Hamming: Recover the practical constraints behind old knowledge before dismissing it. (context: He argues that hunting required useful counts and astronomy required computing even without written records; parties in Jane Austen's time followed the full moon because weak carriage lamps and muddy roads made dark nights difficult. His prehistoric examples are historical inferences, not direct records.)
- [03:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=223s) Hamming: A representation can change what work is practical, even when people resist its unfamiliar ideas. His joke is that Rome fell because Caesar imposed a "3% sales tax" and Roman-numeral arithmetic left no time for anything else. (context: He contrasts cumbersome Roman numerals with the business usefulness of Hindu-Arabic numerals, and describes opposition to zero, a symbol for nothing; the fall-of-Rome story is a joke, not a historical explanation.)
- [05:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=349s) Hamming: Judge a computing tool by useful accuracy and what skilled users can do with it. The slide-rule joke has an engineer multiply two by three and "get 5.99"; he says its repeaters had not mastered the instrument. (context: Adding logarithmic lengths implements multiplication. Even an elaborate cylindrical rule he tried yielded only about four to four-and-a-half significant figures; hand calculators later destroyed the market for the engineer's belt-worn badge.)
- [08:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=537s) Hamming: Computing should change the design you choose. (context: Using a differential analyzer for early Nike missile trajectories, he found that launching upward and attacking from above, rather than launching slantwise, gave more maneuverability with wings about a third as large. This is his account of the design result, with the dates uncertain in his telling.)
- [10:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=600s) Hamming: Reuse engineering that has already survived difficult operating conditions. (context: Condemned M9 gun directors supplied temperature-compensated, fungus-protected components for a general-purpose analog analyzer wired through a telephone patchboard. It proved useful enough that he had a second built, usable alone or joined to the first.)
- [10:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=631s) Hamming: A useful machine can still be wrong for the accuracy a job needs: "how important analog computers are". (context: He praises their actual work but calls them troublesome and limited to roughly four decimal places, whereas some contemporary jobs needed eight or ten. This is his assessment of the analog machines he discusses, not a proved limit on every analog technique.)
- [13:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=788s) Hamming: Remove the human error path all the way to the output. (context: Babbage wanted not just machine-calculated tables but machine-printed numbers, avoiding transcription errors. Repeated differences let polynomial tables be built with additions; local polynomial approximations extended the method's usefulness.)
- [14:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=850s) Hamming: The better machine you imagine can displace the one you have not finished. (context: Babbage moved from the difference engine to a general-purpose computer and completed neither. Hamming also says "the technology was not equal to it" and doubts that a better temperament would have been enough, so he does not explain the failure by ambition or temperament alone.)
- [16:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=976s) Hamming: Build accuracy into the mechanism rather than depend on consistent human force. (context: On the Comptometer, pressing keys prepared the operation but the spring-controlled release performed the addition: "it didn't matter how hard you hit it". Repeated additions and shifting then made multiplication possible.)
- [18:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1101s) Hamming: Anticipate when a growing workload will outrun the existing method. (context: In his account, census workers saw that the 1890 census would take more than the ten years available before the next census. That projected failure of hand methods prompted Hollerith's punched-card mechanization.)
- [19:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1196s) Hamming: Distinguish equipment owned from equipment actually used. (context: He used a university harmonic analyzer, then returned about ten years later and found its display and pen apparently untouched: "the machine was there but it was not being used". By contrast, he treats IBM's roughly 1,500 rented multiplying punches as evidence of use because customers could stop paying rent.)
- [23:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1384s) Hamming: Judge an achievement in light of its obstacles and contributions beyond one specialty. (context: Of Stibitz, Aiken, and Zuse, whom he had met, he personally rates Zuse highest, considering wartime bombing and Zuse's work in both hardware and software. In his telling, inability to keep building machines helped push Zuse toward using them.)
- [23:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1416s) Hamming: Plan around sustained throughput, not a brief performance under pressure: "week in and week out" is the relevant test. (context: A person with a desk calculator might work two or three times faster under duress, but calls, breaks, and ordinary work reduced his practical estimate to about one operation per twenty seconds. He explicitly applies it to men as well as women; the later caption's “26” conflicts with the repeated twenty-second estimate.)
- [25:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1544s) Hamming: Translate enormous performance numbers into human experience before deciding what machines make possible. (context: At roughly a billion multiplications per second, a machine does in about three seconds as many multiplications as a person doing one each second, day and night, for a hundred years—and, he adds, would "probably get them all right". This is a scale analogy, using rounded numbers.)
- [26:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1575s) Hamming: Faster computation requires shorter physical communication paths, not just faster arithmetic. (context: Light travels about a foot in a nanosecond and roughly a hundredth of an inch in a picosecond; otherwise time is spent getting the pulse from one part to another. He follows this with a rough impurity-atom estimate for transistor size, a technology-dependent argument rather than a universal minimum device size.)
- [28:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1700s) Hamming: Account for heat as well as signal speed when packing more computation into less space: "heat is a real problem". (context: More components and more state changes per second concentrate heat; he discusses diamond-like heat spreaders. He doubts reversible computing will solve this in his lifetime, but explicitly says it is not impossible.)
- [29:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1761s) Hamming: More arithmetic units do not automatically repay their cost in speed: "you can't get your money back". (context: He suggests four units might yield about three times one unit's speed, and eight perhaps five, often only on carefully chosen problems. These are his illustrative estimates for extending a von Neumann-type machine, motivating a different architecture rather than universal limits on parallel speedup.)
- [30:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1825s) Hamming: Separate a curve that fits past machines from a theoretical bound: "it's experimental it's no Theory at all". (context: He extended a Los Alamos performance curve years later and found a newer Japanese machine near it. He uses its predicted single-processor saturation, together with signal travel, atomic size, and heat, to illustrate S-shaped growth; the caption garbles the numerical asymptote, so no exact ceiling is asserted here.)
- [31:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=1887s) Hamming: "Never never let your boss say no": make the need visible before inviting a refusal that becomes hard to reverse. (context: He rented outside computer time, with the requesting groups paying the bills, until his boss proposed buying an internal machine to save money. This is his organizational tactic, not a claim that every boss refuses to reconsider; he names John Pierce as an exception who could say "you're right I was wrong go ahead".)
- [33:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2010s) Hamming: "The purpose of computing is Insight not numbers": judge the work by important understanding, not operations performed or results produced. (context: His boss redirected him from maximizing computing-center output to counting "micro Nobel prizes". Understanding lets work move beyond its current technological level; he allows that some computations do simply supply design factors. His friend's joke was "the purpose of computing numbers is not yet in sight".)
- [34:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2072s) Hamming: Distinguish a technological choice from a logical necessity: "our technology dictates a binary system". (context: His group could build ten-state vacuum tubes but could not make them both fast and reliable. Two-state devices won on those engineering requirements, not because he had established that other number systems were impossible.)
- [36:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2195s) Hamming: Keep the instruction-level mechanism distinct from the meaning people assign to its results: "we are the people who put the meaning into the bits". (context: He walks through fetching, decoding, executing, and advancing or replacing the current address. His “bing bing bing” machine follows local operations without a larger picture; he then asks whether a molecular account of humans leaves us in an analogous position, rather than treating that question as settled.)
- [39:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2351s) Hamming: Your beliefs about machine capabilities shape the uses you will attempt. If you think machines can do very little, "you will not be a leader"; if you think they can do anything humans can, he warns that "you'll fall flat in your face". (context: He asks whether people are merely machines and what evidence could distinguish the alternatives; these are his cautions before the AI lectures, not demonstrated capability boundaries.)
- [41:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2474s) Hamming: A claim of awareness needs more than the claimant's assertion. (context: He could make a machine print "I am self-aware" and expects students would not believe it; he then asks why their own assertion should establish self-awareness. The point is to demand grounds for the human-machine distinction, not to prove either side by declaration.)
- [41:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2506s) Hamming: Focus on "what can man and machine together do that we want done", rather than competition between them. (context: He says he defended this position against his bosses for over forty years. A machine's "freedom from boredom" lets it monitor for three years and react to a rare blip; a human watcher cannot be expected to sustain that attention. He still allows that substitution sometimes makes sense.)
- [42:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2569s) Hamming: "Get it clear in your head what you think and also be able to express it clearly"; "quoting me is not legitimate". (context: Asked whether machines can think, students must answer coherently, not agree with him. He takes the opposite side to force reasoning: "you are responsible for your beliefs"; his responsibility is to make them think, not choose their beliefs.)
- [44:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5VItZ6qFyo&t=2663s) Hamming: Reconsider habits learned under old resource limits, including an expert's own habits. (context: He remembers months of round-the-clock atomic-bomb calculations at about one operation per second and says a contemporary machine could do that work in about two seconds. He still thinks of old time-saving tricks, but "you have to save a million of them to save one second" when saving microseconds. His point is the changed payoff of optimization; he admits that his past makes him poorly oriented to guide their future.)
