# Lecture 5 - History of Computers - Applications · notes
- [01:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=71s) Hamming: Communicating is part of creating; speak while the decision is still open. Master writing, formal talks, and informal explanations "at the drop of a hat". (context: His "backroom scientists" waited until two weeks after a committee decision to issue a memo explaining why it was wrong, instead of making their case at the meeting.)
- [02:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=133s) Hamming: Fix a weakness by practicing it on purpose: "I have to accept giving talks even if I don't like to". Make each talk good enough to earn another chance. (context: Large audiences frightened him despite his college teaching experience. One IBM evening talk led to about three talks a year for six to eight years and all the practice he wanted.)
- [03:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=196s) Hamming: Choose the audience's question rather than automatically presenting your own specialty: "what is it they want to hear". (context: For the IBM audience he chose the history of computing to the year 2000. He distinguishes communicating useful material from merely entertaining; he says a friend became so occupied with entertainment that he was pushed out of computing.)
- [04:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=260s) Hamming: Choose duties that keep teaching you: "when you give talks when possible you should profit by it also". (context: Repeating his future-of-computing talk forced him to track developments and new possibilities. He calls this, to some extent, "making my luck".)
- [05:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=321s) Hamming: Explain through things the listener can reason about: "I reduce it down to things you could understand". (context: He replaced beautiful noise and quantum-uncertainty curves with molecular size, impurities, the speed of light, and heat to explain hardware limits.)
- [05:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=352s) Hamming: Look beyond technical possibility to what people can afford to use. (context: His forecast grew from hardware to software and then applications: "a great deal depends upon economics", though he explicitly says this is not the whole explanation. Early machines mainly crunched numbers because those users could afford the bill; falling costs opened other uses.)
- [08:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=508s) Hamming: Maximizing machine use can waste people: "it's machine efficient but human inefficient". Provide spare capacity so people need not wait. (context: His machine sometimes reached 97% utilization for the week. After the telephone analogy failed, he reported his study of "men's John's": he had never seen one without some free capacity. This was his provocative analogy, not a universal claim about queues.)
- [10:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=633s) Hamming: Expect a new method to face heroic tests before being allowed to do routine useful work: it must work under "the worst possible ones" of conditions. (context: He solved difficult partial differential equations on primitive relay equipment, then worked on simpler trajectories, integration, function evaluation, and arithmetic as machines improved. He presents this as a recurring barrier to adoption.)
- [11:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=695s) Hamming: Build for the recurring class of jobs: "mass production of a variable product". (context: He wanted to handle next year's unknown problems by anticipating their categories. One man-year spent on IBM 650 software produced more work within a year than solving jobs separately. Because that machine lasted only two and a half years, he warns that savings should arrive within a year or a year and a half; this is advice from that rapidly changing setting.)
- [13:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=789s) Hamming: A workload can saturate because people cannot propose and absorb answers indefinitely, while new kinds of work sustain total demand. (context: A thousand scientists have limited attention even if they ask bigger questions. At Bell Labs he saw scientific computing flatten, then engineering, military work, and symbol manipulation add demand. Pattern recognition and virtual reality were his proposed next consumers, not established future outcomes.)
- [15:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=945s) Hamming: Judge an unusual proposal on its merits, even when its author seems eccentric: "he's crazy but he's got good ideas". (context: Asked whether Jack Kane could attach a small computer to the Brookhaven accelerator, Hamming talked with him and examined the machine before endorsing the project with some guidance. "Crazy" is Hamming's characterization, not a diagnosis.)
- [16:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=976s) Hamming: A technical recommendation includes practical questions beyond your specialty: "it's a question which he asks I must answer". (context: His vice president also wanted to know whether the computer supplier would survive. Hamming studied manufacturing and economics, questioned its president about capital, and inspected production after looking at other manufacturers; technical feasibility alone did not settle the purchase.)
- [18:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1099s) Hamming: Size up resistance and spend your effort where it can accomplish something. (context: When a department head insisted on unanimous agreement about the next machine, Hamming asked whether he would "settle for the stupidest mind of the bunch", walked out, and chose more constructive work. He had already advised at 11:03 that sometimes "it's better to abandon the idea and go do something else"; he also notes he had unusual freedom to leave this assignment.)
- [19:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1161s) Hamming: Fast feedback can increase the useful output of expensive equipment without replacing it. (context: Online data reduction exposed a sample outside the beam or a suspicious end of a spectrum, so researchers could stop and adjust. Previously they might waste an 18-hour run before examining the data. His estimate was that the small computer "probably" doubled the accelerator's effective capacity, not a demonstrated doubling of its physical capacity.)
- [20:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1256s) Hamming: Buy flexibility because "the presence of the machine changed the job". (context: Laboratory computers first reduced data, then generated electrical stimuli, then closed the feedback loop to adjust experiments while running. General-purpose machines let researchers follow these unplanned uses; special-purpose machines made that harder. He points to interactive experiments on the eye and its feedback response as one result.)
- [23:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1413s) Hamming: Hold the background fixed when judging a change, then integrate the result. (context: Boeing executives wanted everyone using the current airplane design tape. A wing group could mistake another group's improvement for its own, even when its own change made things worse. Groups actually froze a copy for optimization, then reconciled their changes with the rest of the plane. His objection concerns uncontrolled changing inputs during comparison, not databases in general.)
- [24:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1477s) Hamming: Match the freshness of information to the decision and the level that should make it. (context: Executives wanted the complete company state at every moment. He asks whether a decision depending on "moment to moment data" belongs with the president or board: their concern should be long-term effects, while many immediate decisions belong lower down. This is his management argument, not a claim that leaders never need current data.)
- [25:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1540s) Hamming: The same program does not ensure comparable reports when its input changes. (context: One vice president runs Friday afternoon, another Sunday night after weekend data arrives, and both bring different curves to Monday's meeting. He extends the warning to scientific studies: a silently changed value of the speed of light can change results independently of the model being tested, while choosing whose measurement enters the database can itself consume company time.)
- [27:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1665s) Hamming: Design change management around what busy users can actually follow: "blaming the users for not paying attention does not decrease the waste of time of the company". (context: Bell Labs sent two or three software change notices a day. A user encountered a failure weeks later and was told the change had already been announced; assigning blame did not recover the lost work.)
- [28:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1696s) Hamming: Separate a common machine from its variable programs, and count replacement costs before choosing custom hardware. (context: In his Intel story, one production run of a programmable four-bit chip replaced several special-purpose runs. He contrasts this with a broken washing-machine chip obtainable from one source and warns that the "egotism" of a special chip can conceal maintenance problems. These are his historical account and design warning, not a ban on specialized hardware.)
- [31:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1884s) Hamming: Automation may require changing the physical task so it works reliably: "it's got to work on every damn one". (context: Watch the small adjustments you make when a nut sticks on a bolt before assuming the program is simple. He explains the move toward riveting and welding partly through this difficulty; success on many assemblies is insufficient for the production process he describes.)
- [32:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=1945s) Hamming: Study which computer applications succeed and fail, and question inherited rules about what must remain manual. (context: He recalls predicting a reversal from roughly one computer experiment in ten to nine in ten; management dismissed the "crazy long-haired theoretician". The numerical story and the claim that management wasted money are his retrospective account. He urges patience with seniors: their route to the top suited its time and may not suit yours.)
- [35:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2103s) Hamming: Trying to program an activity exposes gaps in what you thought you understood: "I know what I mean by meaning until I ask and then I found I don't know". (context: He invokes Augustine on time while discussing early Russian-to-English translation. He says he can program symbol manipulation but cannot say what meaning is; this expresses his difficulty, not a proof that machines cannot handle meaning.)
- [35:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2133s) Hamming: Successful tests give evidence of understanding, not proof: "that's not proof but that's the evidence we use". (context: He gives three or four integration-by-parts problems and judges whether the student responds as he would. That samples only four out of hundreds of thousands of possible questions; he returns to the same uncertainty at the lecture's end.)
- [37:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2256s) Hamming: Make the useful question "what can machines and us together do". (context: He would like dangerous jobs replaced, but usually concentrates on combined human and machine ability. Asking whether machines can think captures only part of the applications problem.)
- [38:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2319s) Hamming: Start with the simplest case you would accept, and question a formulation that produces no progress. (context: Instead of beginning with Turing's demanding conversational test, he sought the smallest program he would call thinking, small enough that neither half would qualify. Like Galileo slowing motion on a slope to study it, he wanted a tractable starting point. After about a year, perhaps 200 nights of thinking before sleep, he had no answer and wondered whether it was the wrong question; he does not resolve it.)
- [40:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2414s) Hamming: Do not choose a definition merely to protect your own status. (context: He jokes that one could ask "is there intelligent life on Earth" and have trouble proving it. He challenges the wish for a definition of thinking that automatically certifies ourselves, and again asks how small an activity we would accept as thinking.)
- [40:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2444s) Hamming: Keep forecasts answerable to evidence without replacing overconfidence with certainty that progress is impossible. (context: He recalls RAND researchers predicting a computer chess champion within ten years and calls AI full of "wild claims". Speaking in 1995, he also says it would be dangerous to rule out machines beating humans before 2000. His backgammon tournament story is explicitly something he heard, not evidence he personally checked.)
- [43:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2607s) Hamming: Interpret what a person is doing with words, not only their literal content: "I've had my second cup of coffee I'm fit to talk to". (context: That was his eventual reading of his wife's remark that it was raining outside their visible garden. At first he thought, "does that dumb Dame think that I can't see"; the joke is his mistaken literal interpretation. Likewise a passing greeting acknowledges someone rather than requesting a list of ailments, and his earlier shopping example shows why a bare yes or no needs conversational context. He uses these stories to challenge the idea that natural-language programming removes ambiguity.)
- [45:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au6kopvPKCI&t=2732s) Hamming: Naming an example does not specify which abstraction a learner should make. (context: Point at a horse and say horse: is that its name, its color, a four-legged animal, or the class of horses? Children nonetheless learn a first language without grammar lessons, often making forms too regular. He finds the mechanism baffling, while stressing that observable learning and communication still occur; the captions garble his irregular-plural example, so its exact wording is not quoted.)
